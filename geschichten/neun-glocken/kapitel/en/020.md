# Chapter 20: The Bell I Rang

[Hwang Ryeom|friendly, calm] Let go of the rope, boy.

He stood three paces away, between the two bells, hands in his sleeves. He hadn't drawn a weapon. He didn't need one. Mok Yeon heard it in the way he stood. In the calm. In the nothing beneath his voice, the smooth grey surface no light fell on.

[Hwang Ryeom|continuing, almost gently] The Patriarch said you're sixteen. He said it twice. I understood what he meant.

Down in the courtyard Do-yun roared. Steel struck steel. Somewhere on the wall someone screamed, short and high, and stopped.

[Hwang Ryeom|quietly] I'll make you an offer. Just one. Take the little one. Crawl into the bell with him. Stay there until it's light. I'll see to it that no one looks. And in the morning you crawl out, and you live.

Mok Yeon looked at him.

He almost laughed. It was so exact. So perfectly exact. As if someone had written down the first life and handed it to the man in grey to read aloud. Crawl into the bell. Stay there. In the morning you crawl out.

He felt Chil's hands on his leg. They were shaking.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I've done that once already.

Hwang Ryeom raised an eyebrow. Very slightly. That was all the surprise he ever allowed himself.

Mok Yeon laid his palm on Dawn.

***

He listened.

In the middle of the fight, with a murderer three paces away, with the roaring at the gate and the clashing at the wall and Chil sobbing at his leg. He closed his eyes and listened to the bell, the way he had learned that night before he rang it right for the first time.

The bell wasn't still. The noise in the courtyard set it trembling, very finely, every scream, every blow. It swayed. Restless. Like an animal smelling fire.

He waited for the point. The one heartbeat in which it neither came nor went.

Hwang Ryeom moved.

Mok Yeon didn't hear it. He only felt it, as a draft where a moment before there had been none. A knife, narrow and short, out of a grey sleeve. It didn't go for his throat. It went for his hand. The right one, holding the rope.

He didn't let go of the rope.

The blade cut across the back of his hand, deep, to the bone. It didn't hurt. Not yet. It was only hot. Blood ran over his fingers, over the rope, and the rope grew slick.

He pulled anyway.

Dawn spoke.

He had rung the bell correctly yesterday. He had rung it correctly every morning for the last sixteen days. But he had never rung it like this. He put everything into it. The ninth breath and the tenth. The Third Tone that lived in his flesh now. The forty-one years. The night in the bronze. The rain on the steps. He put in everything he had, and then something more that he didn't have, that he borrowed from somewhere, from the old man he had once been.

The tone didn't come out of the bell. It came through it.

He heard Hwang Ryeom take a step back.

And then he heard something he hadn't expected. A rope pulling taut. Beside him. On the other bell.

Chil had grabbed Dew's rope. With both hands. He was hanging from it with his whole round weight, feet off the floor, face wet with tears, and pulling.

Dew answered.

Bright and high, like water falling from a leaf. And between them, out of the air, the third tone.

[Bang Chil|sobbing, shouting] I'm ringing! I can ring! I'm a guard!

Mok Yeon pulled again. Dawn. Chil pulled. Dew. Dawn. Dew. Fast, alternating, over and over, the agreed signal that Madam Ok had heard, that all of Hacheon was meant to hear. The two bells swung against each other, and the third tone between them grew louder, and louder, and it didn't stay in the tower.

It went out.

***

The sound net answered.

At first Mok Yeon heard it only as a hint. A hum at the edge, like a beehive inside a wall. Then clearer. The deepest little bell at the side gate. The one at the west wall. The ones on the goat path, one after another, down the slope to the stream. The ones in the trees along the main path. Forty-seven little voices, tuned by Dal, each different, each right. No one touched them. They answered the great bells the way Dew had answered Dawn, the way the little bell at Seol-a's throat had answered every morning, two days' journey away.

The whole mountain began to sing.

Mok Yeon stood in the middle of it. He didn't just hear it. He was inside it, like the night Do-yun walked down the goat path, only a hundred times stronger. He felt every bell like a finger on his own hand. He felt where the men with the lanterns stood, because the bells beside them sounded different, muffled by their bodies, by their qi. He felt the qi itself. Twenty men, twenty currents in twenty bodies, each swinging in its own rhythm, and around them the mountain sang in another.

And he did what he had done in the tinsmith's passage. Only bigger.

He held the tenth breath. The pause in which nothing happens. The bell that has been struck and has not yet sounded. And he laid the pause into the tone.

The mountain held its breath.

For one heartbeat. A single one. All forty-seven little bells and the two great ones stopped at once, in mid-swing, as if someone had laid a hand on all of them.

And in that silence, the qi of twenty men stumbled.

Mok Yeon heard it. He heard it in the courtyard, at the wall, at the gate, on the goat path. Twenty currents that suddenly had no rhythm, because the rhythm around them had vanished. Like dancers whose music stops mid-step. Men reeled. One fell from the west wall. At the gate someone dropped his saber.

And the five on the mountain who knew what was coming, because the Master had drilled it into them every evening, when the mountain sings, hold your breath, the tenth pause, held their breath.

And when the mountain sang on, they were the only ones who knew where the beat was.

***

The blood came from both ears. Mok Yeon felt it, warm, running down his neck, like the night at the tinsmith's, only more. Much more. The whine in his left ear wasn't a whine anymore. It was a scream.

He was still standing. He was still holding the rope. He didn't know how.

Hwang Ryeom stood in front of him.

He had swayed. Mok Yeon had heard it, very briefly, a stumble in the grey nothing beneath his voice, like a crack in ice. But he hadn't fallen. He stood with one hand on a beam, the short knife in the other. Beneath the village schoolteacher's friendly face, for the first time, something showed. Not anger. Something like wonder.

[Hwang Ryeom|quietly, almost reverent] What are you?

Mok Yeon couldn't answer. He could barely hold the rope anymore. His hand was wet and numb, and his knees wanted to give, like that time on the training ground when he told Seol-a: My body is bad.

Hwang Ryeom raised the knife.

Not at him. At the rope.

And from the ladder came a voice, rough and deep and perfectly calm.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, sharp] That's my bell.

***

The Master came over the edge of the tower floor as if she had grown up there.

She had her sword in her hand. Mok Yeon had seen it on her only once since he woke, the morning she meant to fetch Seol-a, and he had never seen her draw it. It was a plain sword. Straight, unadorned, with a grip of worn leather. There was blood on the blade.

Hwang Ryeom turned to face her. Slowly.

[Hwang Ryeom|polite, almost pleased] Sect Leader Seo. I've hoped we would meet properly one day.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] I haven't.

She attacked.

Mok Yeon had never seen the Master fight. Not in this life and not in the other. In the first life he had only heard her, through bronze, and then not anymore.

She fought the way she taught. Unhurried, unpitying, and without a single unnecessary movement. The Bell Strike Sword, the nine steps, slow and heavy. Each cut a single blow, like a clapper against bronze. Hwang Ryeom dodged, faster than she was, much faster, his short knife darting into the gaps between her cuts like a snake between stones.

But there were no gaps.

She had no hollow steps. Every one of her nine cuts rang. And as he leaned against the bell, bleeding, Mok Yeon understood why people thought the Bell Strike Sword was a drill for beginners. Because they had never seen it done by someone who had practiced it for forty years.

Hwang Ryeom hit her. On the arm. On the side. Once above the hip, deep. She bled. She didn't stop.

She drove him back. Between the two bells. To the edge of the tower, where the side wall was open and the night lay beyond it, and far below, the courtyard.

And there he stopped. He retreated no further. He lowered the knife and looked at her, and beneath his voice there was nothing again, the grey surface, the water in the well.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] You can't hear me. That frightens you. For thirty years you've heard everyone, and you can't hear me.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|panting] I don't need to hear you.

She dropped her sword.

It clattered onto the boards. Hwang Ryeom's eyes followed it, for a hair's breadth. And in that hair's breadth the Master stepped beside Dawn, laid her cheek against the bronze, against the pale patch where generations of disciples had rested their foreheads, and struck the bell with her open palm.

Not with the rope. With her hand. With everything she had. With the Fourth Tone and the Fifth, which she had never shown anyone, and with forty years and a man named Ryu Gang and four sold bells.

The bell screamed.

Mok Yeon had never heard a bell scream like that. It wasn't a tone anymore. It was a wave that burst out of the bronze and tore through the tower like a storm through an open window, and it struck Hwang Ryeom, standing at the edge, full in the chest.

He flew.

He flew backward over the edge of the tower without a sound, grey sleeves fluttering, and then he was gone, and far below in the courtyard Mok Yeon heard an impact. Not loud. Muffled. Like a sack of rice falling from a cart.

The Master stood at the bell. Her cheek still rested against the bronze. She didn't move.

Then she lifted her head from the bell, very slowly, and Mok Yeon saw her face. Blood was running from her right ear. Not like his, a thread. A stream.

She looked at him. She said something. He saw her lips. He heard her, because his own hearing wasn't quite gone yet, but she herself, he could see it in her eyes, didn't hear her own words.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|far too loud, without noticing] KEEP RINGING.

***

He kept ringing. With Chil. Dawn, Dew, Dawn, Dew.

And below, on the road from Hacheon, lights were moving.

Not black. Yellow. Red. White. Lanterns, ordinary lanterns, hundreds of them, that had left the riverbank and were now coming up the road, like a river of light flowing uphill. Mok Yeon heard them. He heard shouts. He heard the clatter of spears: the town watch. He heard a woman's drawling voice bellowing at people to run faster, and the click of jade rings. He heard a young woman screaming a name over and over, and the name was Do-yun.

And ahead of them all, far ahead, at a gallop, a single horse.

It came up the steps. Mok Yeon heard the hooves on stone, impossible, no horse could climb these steps, but this one did, driven, tormented, and on its back a rider who was shouting.

[Cheon Ui-jin|desperate, from the steps] SEOL-A! SEOL-A!

He rode through the broken main gate into the courtyard. Right between the men in black. Right among the black lanterns lying on the ground, torn, in the moonlight. He wrenched the horse around. He saw them. He saw the men. He saw Do-yun leaning with his back against the gatepost, bleeding, sword still raised, the roar finally silent, not because he was dead but because no one was coming anymore. He saw Seol-a on the west wall, sword in hand, with blood on it that wasn't hers.

One of the men in black turned toward him. A man with a nose that had healed crooked. He saw the rider's face in the moonlight.

He froze.

[Lantern Man|flat, horrified] The son.

In the courtyard beside the tower, where Hwang Ryeom had fallen, something moved. A grey shadow rising slowly, bent, one hand on its chest. It saw the rider. It saw the lights on the road.

It whistled. A single short, quiet whistle.

And the men with the black lanterns vanished. They didn't run. They dissolved, like mist when the sun comes. Over the wall. Through the side gate. Down the goat path, where the little bells rang once more, one after another, down the slope to the stream.

Then it was still.

***

In the courtyard lay two men who would not get up again. One at the west wall, below where Seol-a had stood. One at the foot of the wall, his neck broken.

Cheon Ui-jin stood in the middle of the courtyard beside his panting, sweat-soaked horse, holding something he had picked up off the ground. A lantern of black paper. Torn. He turned it slowly in his hands, as if he couldn't grasp what it was.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quietly, confused] What happened here?

No one answered him.

Seol-a came down from the wall. Slowly. She stopped in front of him, sword still in her hand. He looked at her. He saw the blood on the sword. He saw her face.

[Cheon Ui-jin|whispering] Seol-a. What happened here?

She looked at him, and from the tower Mok Yeon saw her open her mouth and close it again. She couldn't tell him. Not here. Not like this.

The first lights came through the gate. Porters with torches. The town watch with spears. Madam Ok in her green silk robe, hair disheveled, out of breath, ten rings glittering in the torchlight. And a young woman with a headscarf and a lantern shaped like a rabbit, who ran past all of them, straight across the courtyard, to the gatepost where a broad man sat bleeding and grinning.

[Min-ji|sobbing, furious] You idiot! You idiot! You idiot!

[Han Do-yun|grinning, weak] I promised, didn't I.

***

Morning came grey and cold.

Mok Yeon sat in the tower beneath Dawn, his back against the beam. Dal had bandaged his hand with a face like stone, ignoring his own burns on the forearm where the boiling water had caught him too. Down in the courtyard he heard voices. The town watch asking questions. The Master answering too loudly without noticing. Bandits, she said. With black lanterns. No, she didn't know who had sent them.

Ui-jin sat on the steps of the main hall, the torn lantern still in his hands, and Seol-a sat beside him, a hand's width apart, and neither of them said anything.

Chil sat beside Mok Yeon. He hadn't moved away from him all night. Sometime toward morning he had stopped crying. Now he just sat there looking at his hands. The palms were torn open from the rope.

[Bang Chil|very quietly] I opened the gate.

[Mok Yeon|after a while] Yes.

[Bang Chil|barely audible] They got in because of me. The Master has a broken ear because of me. Because of me…

He couldn't go on.

Mok Yeon looked at him. The round face. The mole. The torn hands. He heard his heart, small and fast and full of guilt, like a bird that has flown into a window.

He knew this feeling. He had carried it for forty-one years.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Chil. You think I'm a bell ghost.

Chil nodded. Sniffling.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I'll tell you how I became one. It was a night like tonight. Men came with black lanterns. And I hid. Here. In this bell. I made myself very small and stayed inside until morning. And when I came out, no one was left. No one at all.

Chil looked at him. With wide eyes.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] You opened a gate because you wanted to catch a thief. I hid in a bell because I was afraid. And then you took the rope and rang, with torn hands, while a murderer stood beside you. Back then I didn't ring.

He reached out his bandaged hand and laid it on Chil's head, the way Do-yun had done with him.

[Mok Yeon|calm] We'll carry it together. You and me. Then it's only half as heavy.

Chil looked at him for a long time. Then he leaned his head against Mok Yeon's shoulder and stayed that way and said nothing more.

***

When the sun rose above the mist, Mok Yeon stood up.

His legs were shaking. His ears were full of cotton and whining, and on the left he could hear almost nothing. He didn't know if it would come back. He laid his bandaged hand on Dawn, on the pale patch, and waited.

Down in the courtyard it had gone quiet. The town watch had left. Madam Ok sat on a stool in front of the kitchen drinking Dal's ginger water. Min-ji was holding Do-yun's hand. Ui-jin and Seol-a sat on the steps. The Master stood in the middle of the courtyard, among the moss and dandelions, one hand at her right ear, looking up at the tower.

They were alive. All of them.

He listened until the bell came to rest. Until that one point where it neither came nor went.

Then he pulled.

Dawn spoke, deep and full and warm. Dew answered, without anyone touching it. And on the slope, in the trees, on the goat path, at the wall, at the side gate, the little bells sang along, softly, all forty-seven, like a breath a whole mountain lets out after a long night.

It was a good tone. Without a splinter. Without an end.

It was the first morning bell he rang after not hiding.
