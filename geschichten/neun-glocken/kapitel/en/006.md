# Chapter 6: Sparrow Step

The Master taught the way other people chop wood: unhurried, unpitying, and without a single unnecessary word.

They stood on the training ground at sunrise, when the mist still lay knee-deep over the stones and the world smelled of wet grass. She made him stand. Just stand, feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, arms in front of his body as if holding an invisible bell. For an hour. When he trembled, she rapped his calf with the pipe stem, not hard, but exactly where it hurt most.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] Your body slept for six years. Now it's waking up and realizing it can't do anything. That's unpleasant. Keep standing.

He kept standing.

It was strange, being taught like this. In his first life he'd never had a teacher, not after that night. He had learned everything from two pages of notes, from memory, and from forty years of trial and error, and most of the errors had cost him something. His left little finger. Two ribs. A year in which he couldn't hear anything in his right ear. Now a woman stood beside him who knew exactly what a body could bear, and who was teaching it to him with a pipe stem.

He wouldn't have thought being hit could feel so good.

On the third morning, Han Do-yun joined them.

He said nothing. He simply stood beside Mok Yeon in the same stance, the invisible bell in front of his broad chest, and stood. The Master looked at him, raised an eyebrow, and said nothing either. Only once, when Do-yun's left knee sagged too far inward, did she tap it with the pipe stem, and he corrected it with a small, satisfied grunt, as if he'd been waiting for exactly that tap.

After that he came every morning.

***

Chil was harder.

Chil thought he was a ghost, and Chil took ghosts very seriously. The morning after the conversation in the tower, Mok Yeon found a rice ball on the bottom rung of the ladder, carefully wrapped in a cabbage leaf, and beside it an incense stick jammed into a crack in the wood, burning crooked. The next morning there was a steamed bun. On the third, half a dried fish.

Offerings. For the bell ghost.

Mok Yeon ate them.

On the fourth morning, as he unwrapped the rice ball, he heard an outraged hiss from the bush beside the tower. He bit into it, chewed slowly, and looked directly at the bush.

[Bang Chil|from the bush, indignant] Ghosts don't eat!

[Mok Yeon|chewing] This one does.

[Bang Chil|crawling out, covered in leaves] Then you're a hungry ghost. That's the worst kind. They eat everything. Even little boys.

[Mok Yeon|thoughtfully] Are you well seasoned?

Chil squeaked and fled.

But he only fled as far as the woodpile. And the next morning there was another rice ball on the ladder, this time with two pieces of pickled radish beside it, and that morning no one hissed from the bush. Instead Chil sat on top of the wall, legs dangling, and watched Mok Yeon eat.

[Bang Chil|critical] Is it good?

[Mok Yeon|earnest] It's the best rice ball I've ever eaten.

[Bang Chil|suspicious, but flattered] You're only saying that so I'll keep feeding you.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] That too.

Chil thought about this. Then he grinned so wide the mole under his eye moved up.

***

It was Chil's walk that caught Mok Yeon's attention.

He had heard it the very first morning, when the boy ran across the courtyard to fetch Dal: light, quick steps, like a sparrow hopping across a roof. He hadn't thought more about it then. But now that he stood for an hour every morning and could do nothing but listen, he heard it again and again.

Chil was round. Chil ate more than Do-yun. Chil tripped over his own feet when he felt watched. But when he wasn't thinking, when he simply ran, he set his feet down as if he didn't want to wake the ground. Outer edge first, then the ball, and the heel barely touched the earth. It was the kind of walk thieves and assassins practiced for years.

On the fifth day, after standing, when the Master had gone and Do-yun was washing off sweat at the well, Mok Yeon called the boy down from the wall.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Come here. Run across the stones. Those.

He pointed at the stepping stones that led from the training ground to the well. Nine flat stones that had worked loose over the decades, so they clattered at every step. Every disciple who had ever lived on this mountain knew the sound. You could hear it from the kitchen.

[Bang Chil|suspicious] Why?

[Mok Yeon|curt] Run so they don't clatter.

Chil ran. The stones clattered. All nine.

[Mok Yeon|patient] Again. Don't think about the stones. Think about something else. Think about Dal chasing you because you stole buns.

[Bang Chil|indignant] I don't steal…

[Mok Yeon|calm] Run.

Chil ran. And this time only three clattered.

He stopped at the well and looked back at the stones as if they had betrayed him. Do-yun had stopped washing and was looking back too, water dripping from his chin.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Again.

They practiced for an hour. Mok Yeon didn't explain much. He said things like: The heel is a guest, not the master of the house. Or: The ground is a baby; you don't want to wake it. Or: Breathe out when your foot lands, not in. Chil understood none of it, and that was exactly why it worked. He didn't think. He ran.

On the twentieth try, not a single stone clattered.

Chil stood at the well staring at his feet. Then at the stones. Then at Mok Yeon. His mouth opened and closed without anything coming out.

[Han Do-yun|slowly, impressed] I didn't hear anything. Nothing at all. I was watching, and I didn't hear anything.

[Bang Chil|whispering] I was quiet.

[Mok Yeon|calm] You were as quiet as a sparrow on a roof. It's called the Sparrow Step.

That was a lie. It didn't have a name. He had just made it up. But Chil's face lit up as if someone had kindled a lantern inside it, and Mok Yeon decided some lies were better than the truth.

[Bang Chil|awed] A Sparrow Step. I have a technique. I have a real technique. Seol-a doesn't have a Sparrow Step. Neither does Do-yun.

[Han Do-yun|good-natured] I'm not a sparrow.

[Bang Chil|magnanimous] You're an ox. Oxen are important too.

For the rest of the day nobody heard Chil. That was the astonishing thing. He would suddenly appear behind people, in the kitchen, in the dining hall, in the latrine, and whisper Sparrow Step into their ears, and Dal dropped a bowl in fright, and Seol-a nearly caught him with her sword. That evening the Master forbade him to use the Sparrow Step indoors, and Chil accepted it like a knighthood.

Mok Yeon watched him and thought that in his first life he hadn't even remembered the way Chil walked. Only that one brief sound.

***

He rang the morning bell every day.

He rang it wrong. Every day a little less wrong, but wrong. The first day after his talk with the Master, the note had been as hoarse as on the morning he woke. By the fourth, only rough. By the seventh it was full and warm, but at the very end it tipped, a small tremor, a splinter of unrest, and Mok Yeon heard it as clearly as a fingernail scraped across porcelain.

The others didn't hear it. Chil thought it sounded very nice. Do-yun nodded approvingly every time. Only the Master said nothing, and when he looked over at her during breakfast, she shook her head ever so slightly.

His body grew along with it. Slowly, but it grew. On the tenth morning he held the stance for a full hour without trembling. On the twelfth he felt the warm thread in his lower belly thicken, no longer a trickle but a stream, flowing through the first meridians without force. The Second Tone held. Not just as memory this time, but in the flesh.

The whine in his left ear stayed. He got used to it the way you get used to a neighbor who breathes too loudly.

***

On the evening of the twelfth day they sat in the kitchen, because it was raining outside.

It was the first real rain since he woke. It drummed on the roof and dripped into the wooden basin in the main hall, and everyone had gathered around Dal's stove because it was warm there and smelled of millet. The Master was smoking. Seol-a was sewing something onto her sword tassel. Do-yun was peeling chestnuts with a knife that looked like a toothpick in his hands. Chil lay on the floor with his head on a flour sack, staring up at the sooty ceiling.

[Bang Chil|dreamily] Dal. Tell about the bells.

[Dal|growling, without turning] I've told it a hundred times.

[Bang Chil|begging] Mok Yeon doesn't know it. He was always too scared to listen. He thought it was a ghost story.

That was true. Mok Yeon remembered it now. As a boy he had always slipped away when Dal told about the bells, because there was a dead man in the story, and because the old man's voice changed when he told it in a way that frightened him. Later he had pieced the story together from the founder's two surviving pages. He had never heard how Dal told it.

Dal stirred the millet for a while. Then he set down the spoon, wiped his scarred hands on his apron, and sat down on a stool, very slowly, like someone whose knees ache in the rain.

He didn't tell it. He sang it.

It was an old song, in a melody with no beginning and no end, the same one he always hummed while cooking, and suddenly Mok Yeon understood that for forty-one years he had remembered it wrong. It wasn't three notes. It was nine.

[Dal|singing, cracked, slow] Dawn wakes the sleepers. Dew wets the grass. Current carries off what has to fall. Stillness hears what no one says. Hearth keeps warm the ones who come home. Dusk lays down the dead. Storm breaks the walls. Snow covers everything. And the Ninth has no name. The Ninth only rings when it is needed.

The rain drummed. No one said anything.

[Dal|no longer singing, quietly] The founder cast them one after another, in a furnace behind the tower where the nettles grow now. It took him nine years. He said one bell is a note, and nine bells are a voice. And with a voice you can say things a single note can't.

[Bang Chil|whispering] And then?

Dal looked at his hands.

[Dal|very quietly] And then came bad years. One bell after another went away. The Ninth was never hung. No one knows where it is. Storm and Snow…

He broke off. The spoon in the pot stood still. The Master took the pipe out of her mouth.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] Dal.

[Dal|rough, almost angry] I melted them. Storm and Snow. Thirty years ago. In the hunger winter, when the Sixth Master didn't know how to feed twenty disciples. He asked me. I was a bell founder. I knew how to make them, so I knew how to break them too. We sold the bronze in Seoan, and the disciples lived through the winter.

He raised his hands. In the firelight from the stove the scars gleamed, brown and red, up past the wrists.

[Dal|bitter] The bronze was still hot when I took it out of the mold. I didn't wait. I didn't want to wait.

For a long time nobody spoke. Seol-a had stopped sewing. Do-yun held a half-peeled chestnut between his fingers and looked at it as if he didn't know how it had gotten there.

Mok Yeon hadn't known. For forty years he had believed Dal's hands were burned in a kitchen fire.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|after a long pause, calm] And I pawned Dew and Hearth. Five years ago. With Madam Ok in Hacheon, for eighty nyang. So the roof wouldn't cave in entirely.

She said it the way you read out a weather report. But her fingers were wrapped very tightly around the pipe bowl.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|continuing, curt] The term runs out on the tenth day of next month. Madam Ok wrote to me. A bronze dealer from Seoan wants to buy them. For melting down.

[Han Do-yun|at once] The silver. From Pyo. That's enough for one.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|sharp] The silver is for rice. We don't eat bells.

[Han Do-yun|quietly, stubborn] Master…

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|final] No.

Do-yun fell silent. Seol-a stared into the fire. Chil had sat up, the flour sack clutched to his chest.

Mok Yeon sat very still on his stool.

He thought about the tenth day of the seventh month. He had never known what happened to Dew and Hearth. In his first life they simply hadn't been there, like the other six, and he had never asked. Sometime during these days, while he ran from a dog and hid behind the woodpile, a wagon carrying two bells had rolled to Seoan, and in some furnace they had become shapeless bronze, door knockers and cooking pots and belt buckles.

Eighty nyang. Thirteen days.

Outside, the rain slowly stopped. Water dripped from the gutter, irregularly, like someone hesitating.

[Mok Yeon|quietly, mostly to himself] We don't eat bells.

The Master looked over at him. He held her gaze. And he knew she could hear what his heart was doing, and he didn't care.

This time, no wagon would roll to Seoan.
