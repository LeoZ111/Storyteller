# Chapter 15: A Gift from Seoan

They came three days after the gathering, in the late morning, just as the mist had lifted.

Chil saw them first. He was sitting on the wall beside the main gate, where he had taken to sitting every morning because Mok Yeon had told him a sparrow needs a high perch, and he came running across the courtyard so fast he forgot to be silent.

[Bang Chil|breathless] People! On the steps! With a banner! And a litter! And the pretty one from the market is with them!

Mok Yeon was standing on the training ground and already heard them. Many footsteps. Even. Four bearers carrying a load, two guards, a servant with short, hurried steps. And two who walked differently from all the rest. One light and impatient, like someone who would like to run and is holding himself back. And one so quiet that Mok Yeon heard him only because he knew he had to.

He knew that step. He had heard it nearly four weeks ago on the goat path, at night, through wet grass.

***

Cheon Ui-jin came through the gate first.

He wore the plain pale blue robe again and held a scroll in both hands, wrapped in a blue silk ribbon. Behind him four men carried a closed litter that wasn't a litter but a chest on poles, long and flat, lacquered. Two guards in white. A young servant looking around in awe, as if he'd been told immortals lived on this mountain and now didn't know what to make of the sagging roof.

And a fifth man, walking beside the servant as if he belonged there.

He wore the robe of a house steward, dark grey, with a narrow blue band on the sleeve, the mark of the Cheon family. He was about forty-five, slender, clean-shaven, with a friendly face that recalled a village schoolteacher. He kept his hands in his sleeves. He looked around. Not like the servant, wonderstruck. He looked around like someone with a map in his head, checking whether it was accurate.

His gaze passed over the west wall. Over the kitchen. Over the bell tower.

Then over Mok Yeon.

He smiled. Politely. And raised a hand, not high, just a little, as if in greeting.

Mok Yeon felt his heart skip a beat and then race on twice as fast. He lowered his head. He bowed the way a disciple bows to the retinue of a great house, and when he straightened up, he didn't look again.

The Master stepped out of the main hall. She had heard them, or Chil, or both. She had her pipe in her belt and a face that was very well rehearsed.

[Cheon Ui-jin|bowing deeply, warm] Sect Leader Seo. Forgive us for arriving unannounced. My father sends me with a message. And with an apology.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|surprised, well played] An apology? Young Master, it's my disciple who must apologize. Not the Cheon family.

She turned. She found Mok Yeon with her eyes across the courtyard, and her look could have curdled milk.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|sharp] Come here. Bow to the young master. Lower. Lower still.

Mok Yeon came. He bowed until his forehead nearly touched the ground. Ui-jin looked embarrassed.

[Cheon Ui-jin|hastily] Please, there's no need. My father laughed when he got home. He says the robe was ugly anyway. He says no one has doused him with tea so heartily in years.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] The boy is a disaster. I've had him scrubbing the latrine every morning for three days.

That was true. Mok Yeon hadn't argued. It had been part of the plan, and besides, the latrine really was filthy.

***

Ui-jin didn't unroll the scroll. He told them what was in it, eyes shining, as if it were his own gift.

The Cheon family was offering the Nine Bells Gate a protection contract. The stretch of road between Hacheon and the west camp, an hour's walk, where refugees had been attacked twice in recent weeks. Two disciples, every other day, until the end of the year. In return, twenty nyang a month. And rice.

Twenty nyang a month. Do-yun, standing at the edge of the courtyard, went very still. Mok Yeon heard his heart leap. Twenty nyang. More than Pyo paid him in half a year.

[Cheon Ui-jin|eager] And that's not all.

He turned and waved to the bearers. They set down the long flat chest in the middle of the courtyard, among the moss and dandelions, and the servant opened the lid.

Inside, on red velvet, lay a piece of wood. A dark, heavy beam of camphor, as long as a man, with an iron hook set into one end. Beside it, carefully wrapped, a chain.

The Master looked at the beam. Her face didn't change. But Mok Yeon heard her breath stop.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quietly, almost solemn] This is the beam from which the bell called Dusk hangs in my family's ancestral hall. My father says it doesn't belong in Seoan. He says he bought it ten years ago because he couldn't forget its sound, but a bell belongs where it was cast. He sends the beam as a promise. The bell itself will come by wagon as soon as you're ready to hang it. As a gift. Without payment.

It was very quiet in the courtyard.

Dal stood in the kitchen doorway. He had his burned hands folded in front of his apron, and he was staring at the beam as if he were seeing a ghost.

Mok Yeon glanced at the man in grey. Only from the corner of his eye. He stood beside the servant, hands in his sleeves, looking at the Master. Not at the beam. Not at Ui-jin. Only at her. Patient. Attentive. The way the Master herself looked at people when she was listening to them.

Mok Yeon understood.

It wasn't a gift. It was a question.

A poor sect leader who heard nothing accepts with gratitude a bell she had to sell out of need ten years ago. She cries, perhaps. She bows to her benefactor's son. She sends for carpenters at once.

A sect leader who heard something takes nothing from the hand of a man with people screaming inside him.

He knew what she wanted to do. He heard it in her heart. She wanted to touch the beam. She wanted to throw it back into the chest. She wanted both at once.

The Master stepped forward.

She knelt beside the chest, slowly, and laid her hand on the beam. On the dark, smooth wood from which her bell had hung for ten years. She left her hand there. And when she raised her head, her eyes were wet, and Mok Yeon didn't know whether she was acting or not. He thought she didn't know either.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|rough, quiet] Tell your father… tell him that ten years ago I believed I would never hear it again.

Ui-jin beamed. He looked as if he might cry himself.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|rising, composing herself, turning practical] But the tower is old. Look at it. The beams are rotten, have been for two years. Dew hangs up there because Dal prays every day. If we hang another bell, the heaviest one at that, the whole tower will come down. We have to replace the beams first. With the silver from your protection contract. After the Mid-Autumn Festival, when the carpenters from Hacheon have time. Then we'll fetch it. With joy.

[Cheon Ui-jin|eager] Of course! My father will understand. He'll even want to pay for the carpenters…

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|smiling, but firm] He won't. The Nine Bells Gate hangs its own bells. A poor sect has to be able to afford that much pride.

Ui-jin laughed warmly and bowed. He took it as pride. It was pride, too.

The man in grey bowed as well. He hadn't stopped watching the Master the whole time. Now he looked away. Up at the tower. At the rotten beams that weren't rotten at all, and the two bells hanging from them, and the seven empty hooks.

***

Afterward, Ui-jin went to Seol-a.

She had stayed on the training ground the whole time, sword in hand, as if she hadn't noticed anything. Now he came to her, and she bowed, and they walked slowly together along the edge of the ground beneath the old pine, talking quietly. Ui-jin laughed often. Seol-a laughed once.

Chil sat on the wall and watched them with the dark look of a boy who has decided not to like someone.

Do-yun stood with the bearers, chatting about the road to Seoan and which inn had the best noodles. Dal had vanished into the kitchen and was clattering pots so loudly it sounded like he was fighting them.

And the man in grey stood alone at the edge of the courtyard, studying the west wall.

Mok Yeon was scrubbing a bucket nearby. He had taken the bucket because he needed something to do with his hands, and because a boy scrubbing the latrine is invisible. He scrubbed and didn't listen. He kept his promise.

He didn't have to listen. Chil did it for him.

He only noticed when the boy was suddenly crouching beside him, out of nowhere, without a single stone clattering, whispering in his ear.

[Bang Chil|whispering, excited] The grey man was talking to himself. I was behind the woodpile. Sparrow Step. He didn't notice anything.

Mok Yeon stopped scrubbing.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] What did he say?

[Bang Chil|proud, whispering] He looked at the wall and said: Still collapsed. And then he looked at the tower and said: The beams aren't rotten. And then he smiled. Like this.

Chil imitated a smile. It was a very good smile. Polite, friendly, a little sad. A chill ran down Mok Yeon's spine.

[Bang Chil|curious] Is he a carpenter? He knows about beams.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause, calm] Chil. Listen to me. If you ever see him again, the grey man, anywhere, in the market, on the path, wherever, you don't go near him. No Sparrow Step. No listening. You turn around and leave. And then you tell me.

[Bang Chil|unsettled] Why?

[Mok Yeon|earnest] Because I'm a bell ghost. And bell ghosts know things.

Chil looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, very seriously, and vanished again without a sound, and Mok Yeon went on scrubbing his bucket, which had long since been clean.

***

They left at midday.

Ui-jin said goodbye to everyone individually, even Dal, who came out of the kitchen and pressed a bowl of millet into his hands for the road with his burned hands, without a word. Even Chil, who stayed sitting on the wall with his arms folded. Even Mok Yeon.

[Cheon Ui-jin|friendly, quiet] My father said to tell you he isn't angry with you. And that you really do ring the bell beautifully.

[Mok Yeon|bowing] Thank you, Young Master.

[Cheon Ui-jin|hesitating, after a pause] Seol-a says you've become strange. But in a good way. She says you showed her something about her fourth step that she hadn't noticed in eleven years.

He smiled. It was a completely open smile. No envy in it, no jealousy, only curiosity.

[Cheon Ui-jin|warmly] Maybe you'll show me too sometime.

Mok Yeon looked at him. He thought of a clear day on a mountain road, a familiar horse, a father sitting by a coffin for three days.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Maybe.

The bearers lifted the empty chest. The beam stayed in the courtyard, leaning against the wall, dark and heavy. The guards went first, then Ui-jin, then the servant. The man in grey went last.

At the gate he stopped, as if something had occurred to him. He turned. Mok Yeon stood alone in the courtyard, bucket in hand.

The man in grey came back. Not quickly. Three steps, four. He stopped in front of Mok Yeon, so close that Mok Yeon could smell ink on his sleeves. He smiled. Polite. Friendly. A little sad.

[Hwang Ryeom|very quietly, only for him] My man sends his regards. The one with the nose. His wrist is healing well.

Mok Yeon gripped the bucket.

[Hwang Ryeom|in the same tone] You have something that belongs to him. Keep it. As a memento.

He bowed. Exactly the way a craftsman bows to someone whose work he respects. Then he turned and left, through the gate, down the forty-seven steps, and Mok Yeon didn't hear his footsteps.

He never heard them.

Behind him, against the wall, leaned the camphor beam. Up in the tower Dawn and Dew hummed in the wind. And in the kitchen, Dal had stopped clattering the pots.

It was very quiet on the mountain.

[Mok Yeon|thoughts] They know I know something. They just don't know what.
