# Chapter 3: Bell Ear

Hacheon smelled of fish, wet timber, and fried dumplings, and it was loud in a way that nearly knocked Mok Yeon over.

For forty-one years he had lived in silence. He had learned that silence is never truly silent, that it is made of wind and water and your own blood. But he had forgotten what it was like when a hundred people talked at once. On the riverbank, fishwives haggled with cooks over silvery carp still twitching in their baskets. A porter cursed a donkey; the donkey brayed back. Dice rattled in a cup inside a teahouse. Somewhere someone was hammering sheet tin, tirelessly, in a rhythm that matched nothing else. And underneath it all, like a deep bass line, the Cheongnyu rushed by, brown and broad, carrying boats with patched sails.

Mok Yeon stopped in the middle of the street and closed his eyes for a moment.

[Han Do-yun|worried] You all right? If you feel sick, sit by the well. I'll come get you after.

[Mok Yeon|slowly] It's loud.

[Han Do-yun|laughing] It's market day. You've been here a hundred times.

A hundred times. And then not for forty-one years. In his first life he had avoided Hacheon like an open wound. He had passed through it once, at fifty, in winter, at night, and had not looked up toward the mountain.

He opened his eyes again and followed Do-yun.

The First Disciple moved through the crowd like an ox through tall grass. He didn't push, didn't shove, and still the way opened in front of him, because people knew him. A vegetable seller greeted him by name. An old woman pressed a roasted ear of corn into his hand and refused to take money, because in spring he had pulled her grandson out of the river. Do-yun took the corn, thanked her awkwardly, and passed it to Mok Yeon the moment she turned away.

[Han Do-yun|quietly] You eat it. You've gotten thin.

Mok Yeon ate. The corn was hot and tasted of charcoal.

***

They stopped at a tofu stall.

Han Do-yun pretended it was an accident. He stopped as if he'd just remembered he needed tofu, though Dal hadn't mentioned any that morning. He studied the white blocks in their water basins with great seriousness. His ears turned red.

Behind the stall stood a young woman with rolled-up sleeves and forearms strong from pressing beans. A round face, a snub nose, a cloth tied over her head with one black strand escaping from under it. She saw Do-yun coming and folded her arms.

[Min-ji|challenging] Ah. The honored martial artist. Going to stare at my tofu for ten minutes again and then buy one piece?

[Han Do-yun|stammering] I, uh. Two pieces. Two today.

[Min-ji|dry] How extravagant.

She wrapped two blocks in a lotus leaf, and when she handed it to him their fingers touched. Do-yun nearly dropped the package. Min-ji saw the scrape over his eyebrow, and her voice changed just a shade.

[Min-ji|quieter] What's that?

[Han Do-yun|evasive] Nothing. Bandits at Black Pine Pass. There were only four.

[Min-ji|sharp] Only four. You idiot.

She said it like a pet name. Mok Yeon stood beside them and listened, not with Bell Ear, just with the ears everyone listens with, and it was clear enough anyway. He heard the First Disciple's heart stumble, heavy and helpless. He heard the young woman hold her breath before she said idiot.

In his first life he had never known about her. Do-yun had never mentioned her. He had simply bought two pieces of tofu every week, or one, and climbed back up the mountain, and on the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival he had held the gate.

Mok Yeon wondered how long she had waited at her stall for him.

[Mok Yeon|earnest, to Min-ji] He won't die.

Both of them turned to him. Min-ji blinked. Do-yun went red to the roots of his hair.

[Min-ji|baffled] Who on earth is that?

[Han Do-yun|hastily] My junior brother in the art. He has a fever. Come on, Mok Yeon. Come on.

***

The Green Current Escort Agency stood by the harbor, a two-story building with a banner showing an arrow flying over waves. In the yard stood three wagons and a dozen men with sabers, playing dice or asleep. In the counting room, behind a table covered in abacuses, sat Agency Chief Pyo, a plump man with a thin mustache that looked painted on in ink.

He smiled when Do-yun came in. It was the smile of a man trying to sell bad news as good.

[Pyo|jovial, with feigned regret] Ah, Han Do-yun! The hero of Black Pine Pass. Sit, sit. Tea? No? Listen, my boy. I have sad news. We lost a bale. Silk from Seoan, the finest. The bandits took it while you were busy with the others. The merchant is demanding compensation. You understand I'll have to cut your wages. Half pay. I truly am sorry.

Do-yun stood in front of the table, his big hands hanging at his sides. Mok Yeon saw his jaw working.

[Han Do-yun|slowly, confused] There were four bandits. I drove off all four. Not one of them got near the wagons. I counted the bales myself before we moved on.

[Pyo|sighing] You miscount in the heat of battle. Happens to the best. And when we reached Hacheon there were eleven, not twelve. I have the list right here.

He slid a sheet across the table. Do-yun looked at it without reading it. He couldn't read well. Mok Yeon knew that now. In his first life, he hadn't.

Half pay. The sect lived on this pay. Mok Yeon thought of the single egg at breakfast.

He closed his eyes.

He knew it would cost him something. The whine in his left ear hadn't gone away since that night, only grown quieter. He did it anyway. Not like that night, not a stone dropped into a pond, but carefully, like a single drop from a spoon. Only a few heartbeats. Only this room.

The world opened a crack.

Pyo's heart beat fast and oily, a fat, well-fed rhythm. At the word silk it gave a small jump. At the word list, another.

And behind the counting room, behind a thin wall, in a storeroom smelling of lamp oil and mice: a creak. Someone was moving crates back there, and in doing so bumped into something wooden on wheels, and one of the wheels squeaked. A very particular squeak. High, then low, like a duck choking.

Mok Yeon opened his eyes. Something warm ran from his nose. He wiped it away with the back of his hand before anyone saw.

[Mok Yeon|calm] He's lying.

Pyo froze. Do-yun turned around slowly, with the face of a man who has just heard his dog speak.

[Pyo|indignant] How dare this brat? Han Do-yun, tell your little brother…

[Mok Yeon|unmoved, to Do-yun] At Black Pine Pass. Did one of the wagons squeak?

[Han Do-yun|bewildered] The rear wagon. Left wheel. I hammered a pine wedge into it, but it squeaked anyway. The whole way. The men wanted to kill me.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] High, then low. Like a duck choking.

Do-yun stared at him.

[Mok Yeon|pointing at the wall] The wagon is in there. Someone's unloading the crates off it right now.

The counting room went very quiet. Out in the yard one of the dice players laughed, and the laugh suddenly sounded far too loud.

Do-yun said nothing. He simply walked to the door in the back wall, past Pyo, who had half risen and raised his hands like someone trying to stop an ox, and opened it. The ox did not stop.

In the storeroom stood a wagon. On the wagon, half hidden under crates, lay a bale wrapped in grey cloth, and at one corner, where the cloth had slipped, it shimmered pale green, like water under ice. A farmhand stood beside it holding a crate, looking like he wanted to hide inside it himself.

Do-yun bent down and examined the left rear wheel. A wedge of pale pine was jammed into the hub. He recognized it. Of course he did. He had carved it with his own hands.

He turned around.

Han Do-yun was not a man who made threats. He had no talent for it. He simply stood in the doorway, and the doorway was suddenly too small for him, and he looked at Pyo, and in that look there was no anger, only a deep, honest disappointment, like a child who has had the same toy taken away for the third time.

[Han Do-yun|quiet, deliberate] You wanted to sell the bale yourself and tell the merchant I lost it.

[Pyo|sweating, hurried] A misunderstanding! A terrible misunderstanding! The farmhand stored it in the wrong place. I'll have him whipped. Of course you'll get your full pay. And a bonus. For the honesty. For our excellent cooperation!

The farmhand dropped the crate.

***

On the way back, Do-yun carried a purse of silver heavier than any he had brought home that year. He carried it in both hands, in front of his belly, the way you carry a chicken you don't want to startle.

For a long time he said nothing. They walked along the river, where the willows trailed their branches in the water and the evening sun turned the Cheongnyu the color of copper.

[Han Do-yun|finally, carefully] How did you hear that? Through a wall. The wheel.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] I listened.

[Han Do-yun|doubtful] That's Bell Ear. That's the Third Tone. It took me three years to get it. Seol-a, two. Last week you didn't even have the first.

Mok Yeon didn't answer. What could he say? Do-yun was a bad liar and a good man, and Mok Yeon didn't want to lie to him. So he stayed silent, and the silence hung between them like the smell of the river.

[Han Do-yun|softly] Your nose is bleeding.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I know.

[Han Do-yun|after a while, awkwardly] Thanks. For earlier. With Pyo. And for what you said to Min-ji. Even if it was strange.

[Mok Yeon|earnest] It wasn't strange. It was a promise.

Do-yun looked at him sideways for so long he nearly tripped over a mooring post. Then he laughed his tumbling-staircase laugh, short and embarrassed, shook his head, and said nothing more. But afterward he walked a little closer beside him, the way you walk beside someone you mean to look out for.

On the main street, where they turned onto the mountain path, a wall was plastered with notices. Wanted bandits. A runaway ox. A reward for a lost ring. And above them, fresh, the paper still bright, in large and beautiful brushstrokes:

On the seventh day of the seventh month, at the River Pavilion of Hacheon, the Patriarch of the Cheon family of Seoan, Cheon Mu-gyeong, the Righteous Blade, will address the sects and families of the region on the protection of refugees from the north. All martial artists of good will are invited.

Mok Yeon stopped.

He read it three times. The calligraphy was very beautiful. He knew whose hand had written it without knowing, the way you recognize a voice.

[Han Do-yun|impressed] The Righteous Blade. In Hacheon. The Master will go. Everyone will go. They say at twenty-five he held off thirty bandits alone so a column of refugees could get over the pass. His meridians tore doing it, and he fought his way back anyway. Imagine that.

[Mok Yeon|flat] Yes. Imagine that.

He tore himself away and walked on. He didn't want Do-yun to see his face.

And then, two buildings further, he saw it.

An inn. An ordinary, shabby inn at the edge of the market called the Willow Branch, whose sign some wind had knocked crooked years ago. Under the jutting eaves, beside a bundle of dried peppers, hung a lantern.

It was made of black paper. It wasn't lit. It was broad daylight.

Mok Yeon stopped breathing.

It had taken him twenty years to understand what a black lantern by day meant. He had learned it from a dying man in a prison in Mujang, who drew the sign in the dust for him before he could no longer open his mouth. A black lantern by day meant: We are here. We have accepted. Whoever needs us, come forward.

In his first life he had walked past this inn every day during these weeks. He had never noticed the lantern.

On the upper floor, a shutter was closing. Slowly. Unhurried. The way someone closes a book once they've found the page they were looking for.

[Han Do-yun|from behind, good-natured] Mok Yeon? What is it? Hungry? I think they have good noodles here.

[Mok Yeon|quietly, without turning around] No. Let's go home.

All the way up the mountain he felt the whine in his ear, high and thin and tireless. And beneath it, deeper, barely there, something he had heard once before, forty-one years ago, through bronze:

Paper crackling in the wind.
