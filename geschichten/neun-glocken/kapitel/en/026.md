# Chapter 26: Bronze

To recast a bell, you first have to break it.

Dal said so on the first morning, when Current lay in the courtyard on two wooden planks, green and huge and cracked, with everyone standing around it. He said it without any feeling in his voice, the way you say rice has to be washed before you cook it. Then he took the heavy hammer Do-yun had forged in the furnace and handed it to Mok Yeon.

[Dal|growling] You.

[Mok Yeon|confused] Me?

[Dal|without looking at him] You can hear it. You know where it'll break. I know too, but my arms are old.

Mok Yeon took the hammer. It was heavy. His right hand, the one with the scar to the bone, ached as he closed it around the grip. He stood before the bell that had lain sixty years in the river, that had answered when Chil called it, and he didn't want to raise the hammer.

[Dal|quietly] It's already broken, boy. You're not breaking it. You're setting it free.

He laid his left hand on the bronze. He listened. He heard the crack running through the metal, and the fine hairline cracks branching off it like branches from a trunk. He heard where the bronze was tired.

He struck there.

The sound was terrible. A shriek that tore across the courtyard and over the wall and down the slope, and the little bells on the goat path answered, startled, bright, jumbled. Chil covered his ears. The Master turned her head away.

The bell broke. A piece of the rim as big as a loaf of bread fell clanging onto the planks.

Mok Yeon stood there, hammer in hand, breathing hard. Then he raised it again.

It took him all day. By evening Current lay in forty-three pieces in the courtyard, and his hand was bleeding through the bandage, and Dal came and took the hammer from him and wordlessly put a bowl of rice with salt into his other hand.

***

The mold took ten days.

Dal built it behind the tower beside the furnace, in a pit Do-yun dug, as deep as a man, with straight walls. In the middle of the pit he set an iron spindle, an upright rod that something could turn around. To the spindle he tied a board.

The board was the most important thing. Mok Yeon understood that on the second day, watching Dal cut it. He cut it from an old door, with a small knife, along a line only he could see. A curved edge, narrow at the top, then bulging, then narrower again, then flaring wide at the bottom. The profile of a bell. The profile of Current.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] How do you know what it has to look like? It's broken.

[Dal|without looking up, carving] I measured it before you broke it. With string. Every hand's width. But that's not the important part.

He held the board up to the light. He sighted along the edge.

[Dal|quietly] The important part is how it has to sound. I heard it when I was eight. Just once, before it left the tower. I was standing down in the courtyard, and the Fifth Master rang it in farewell. I still remember the tone. I've had it in my head for sixty years. The shape has to be such that that tone comes out.

He turned the board around the spindle. Slowly. Once all the way around. And Mok Yeon saw the curved edge describe a bell in the air, a bell made of nothing.

Onto this bell of nothing, Dal spread clay.

Layer by layer. First coarse clay with straw, for the core. Then fine clay mixed with horse dung and hair, so it wouldn't crack. After each layer he turned the board around the spindle, and the board smoothed the clay into exactly the shape it had. Then the clay dried, and the next day came the next layer.

Chil was allowed to mix the clay. Do-yun carried the water. Seol-a kept the fire in the pit burning, a small steady fire that dried the clay from inside. The Master sat at the edge of the pit every evening, smoking and watching and saying nothing.

And Mok Yeon sat beside Dal and listened.

He listened to the clay. He hadn't known clay had a sound. Wet clay was mute; it swallowed everything. But as it dried it began to ring, very faintly, if you tapped it with a knuckle, and Dal tapped it constantly and listened and shook his head and put on another layer. Or nodded and went on.

[Dal|on the fifth evening, tapping] Listen. There. And there.

Mok Yeon listened. Two places on the clay, one up at the shoulder, one down at the rim, that sounded different. Hollow. A little.

[Dal|growling] Air. A bubble in the clay. When the bronze runs in there, it'll be thin there. And a thin place in a bell is like a lie in a sentence. You don't hear it if you're not listening. But once you listen, you don't hear anything else.

He scraped the spots open, refilled them, smoothed them with the board.

Mok Yeon sat there thinking of Seol-a's fourth step.

***

On the tenth evening the mold was finished. Two molds, really. A core that was the inside of the bell, and a cope that was the outside, and between them a gap as thick as a finger for the bronze to flow into. Dal had lifted off the cope, cut out the false clay bell that had sat in between as a placeholder, and set the cope back on. Now a huge cone of fired clay stood in the pit, with an opening at the top through which you looked into darkness.

Do-yun shoveled the pit full. Earth around the mold, tamped hard, so it wouldn't burst when the hot bronze ran in. Only the opening at the top stayed clear.

And then they fired the furnace.

***

They poured at night. Dal insisted. By day, he said, you can't see what color the bronze is, and the color tells you when it's ready.

The furnace had been burning since noon. Do-yun worked the bellows hour after hour with his good leg, in the rhythm of Dal's song, and Mok Yeon took over when Do-yun's leg started shaking, and Seol-a took over from Mok Yeon. In the crucible lay the forty-three pieces of Current and a few cooking pots from Madam Ok's chests, because some of the bronze had been lost in the river. Dal had weighed them, every one, on a scale he'd fetched from the kitchen.

Toward midnight the bronze began to glow.

First red, dark, like embers. Then brighter. Orange. Then yellow. Then a color Mok Yeon had never seen, a yellow shading into white, with green sparks rising from the surface and vanishing. The heat from the furnace struck their faces so that their eyebrows crackled. Dal stood before it without gloves, looking into the crucible.

[Dal|quietly, almost tender] Not yet. Not yet. Listen.

Mok Yeon listened.

The bronze was singing.

He hadn't known molten metal sings. It wasn't a sound you heard with your ears. It was a seething, a trembling, deep down, like Dawn's hum when the wind touched it. But restless. Jumbled. Like a hundred bells all struck at once.

And then, slowly, as the color grew still brighter, the jumble sorted itself out. The hundred tones found one another. They sought each other, the way Dawn and Dew had sought each other, circled one another, and became one.

[Dal|whispering] Now.

***

The three of them lifted the crucible. Dal, Do-yun, and Mok Yeon, with long iron bars through the handles, and the heat was so fierce Mok Yeon felt the hair on his forearms curl. They carried it to the pit, six steps that were as long as the forty-seven. Seol-a went ahead with a torch. The Master stood at the edge of the pit. Chil held a lantern, an ordinary yellow one, and his hand shook so much the light danced.

Dal tipped the crucible.

The bronze flowed.

A stream of light, white-yellow, thick as a wrist, plunging into the mold's opening and vanishing into the dark. It hissed. It steamed. From small holes Dal had pierced in the clay around the opening, flames shot out, blue and green, as the air escaped from the mold. The smell of burned clay and hot metal and something sweet and sharp.

And Dal sang.

Loudly this time. Not hummed but sung, in his cracked old voice, the tune with no beginning and no end. The casting song. Dawn wakes the sleepers. Dew wets the grass. Current carries off what has to fall.

Mok Yeon stood beside him, the iron bar in his hands, and heard the bronze run into the mold. He heard it fill the gap from the bottom up, the way water fills a jug. He heard it reach the rim and the shoulder and the crown. He heard it begin to cool. Slowly. At the edges first.

And as it cooled, it kept singing.

A tone, deep, that carried through the earth, through the tamped pit, through the feet of the people standing around it. It wasn't finished. It was a tone still becoming. And Mok Yeon felt something in his lower belly answer. The flow in his meridians, which had grown wider and calmer since Mid-Autumn night. It swung along. It couldn't help it. He was standing beside a bell being born, and his body heard it the way he heard the bronze, and the bronze heard him.

He closed his eyes.

He heard Dal singing. He heard the Master breathing. He heard Chil's heart, fast, excited. He heard Do-yun's heart, heavy and tired. He heard Seol-a.

And beneath Seol-a's heartbeat he heard something.

Not the screaming the Master had spoken of. Nothing terrible. Something very small. A tone beneath the tone. She stood at the edge of the pit with the torch, looking into the flames, and her face was calm, and her heart beat calmly, and beneath it, very quietly, lay a sadness she showed to no one. He didn't know about what. Maybe about a young man with a face that had nothing beneath it, who had ridden back to Seoan on a lame horse.

He heard it. Beneath the heartbeat. Like the bronze beneath the song.

The Fourth Tone. Undertone.

He opened his eyes. He hadn't used force. No blood ran from his nose. It had simply been there, the way Bell Ear had been there that morning during stance, a door that opened and didn't swing shut.

Beside him, Dal stopped singing. He tilted his head like a bird. He listened to the pit.

[Dal|quiet, tense] There. You hear it?

Mok Yeon heard it. Right at the bottom of the mold, at the rim, on the side facing the slope: a tiny false note. A hollow spot the size of a thumbnail. A bubble. A lie in a sentence.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] At the rim. Toward the slope.

Dal looked at him. For a long time. Then he laughed. It was the first time Mok Yeon had heard him laugh, a rough, creaking laugh like an old door.

[Dal|creaking, amused] You hear the bronze lying. At sixteen.

He looked at the pit. He thought.

[Dal|after a while, calm] Leave it. It lay in a river for sixty years. It's allowed one small lie. At the rim, where nobody sees it. That doesn't make it worse. That just makes it itself.

***

Three days later they dug the bell out.

The clay was black and hard and broke under Do-yun's hammer into large shards. Beneath it the bronze appeared. Not green. Golden. A warm, reddish gold, smooth and gleaming, like honey in the light. New bronze isn't green. It only becomes green over the years, with rain and wind and the hands of disciples resting their foreheads against it.

Dal knelt in the pit and ran his scarred hands over the shoulder. Over the rim. Over the spot facing the slope where the tiny bubble was, barely visible, just a small dark fleck. He smiled.

They lifted it out with the pulley. They set it in the courtyard on the wooden planks where its forty-three pieces had lain. Dal fetched the file. He tuned it for two days. He scraped and struck and listened and scraped, and no one was allowed near except Mok Yeon.

At the end of the second day, Dal put down the file.

He stood before the new bell. He took a wooden clapper. And he looked around the courtyard as if searching for someone.

At the gate stood Old Gong.

Chil had fetched him. He had led him by the hand all the way from Bell Bend, an hour along the river and up the forty-seven steps, one at a time, and the old man had taken as long for each step as others take for ten. Now he stood at the gate, breathing hard, leaning on Chil's shoulder, his milky eyes turned toward the courtyard where he saw nothing.

Dal saw him. He nodded. Then he struck the bell.

Current spoke.

Deep. Deeper than Dawn. Full and round, and at the end of the tone, far down, something flowing, a pull, as if the tone were carrying something away with it. Like a river bending around a rock. The tone filled the courtyard and went over the wall and down the slope, and on the goat path the little bells answered, and in the tower Dawn and Dew hummed without anyone touching them, greeting the sister who had been gone sixty years.

Old Gong stood at the gate with his hands held out in front of him, as if to catch something.

[Old Gong|whispering, cracked] That's it. That's it, the way I heard it before it fell. Exactly.

Dal lowered the clapper. He said nothing. He only stood there, a small, bent old man with burned hands, looking at the golden bell, and something glistened on his cheeks in the evening light.

[Dal|barely audible] Thirty years. Once I mended something.

***

The next day a letter came from the inspector.

Her clerk brought it up the mountain himself, out of breath, and handed it to the Master with a bow deeper than protocol required. It was a copy of her report to the Alliance branch in Seoan. Two pages, in the inspector's narrow, precise hand. The Master read it aloud, too loudly, because she could only half hear herself.

The art of the Nine Bells Gate is not a demonic art within the meaning of Article Nine. It disturbs the qi of others, but it does not steal, poison, or destroy it. The complaint is dismissed.

Chil cheered. Do-yun lifted him and spun him around with his good arm, and Chil shrieked. Dal banged a pot with his wooden spoon.

The Master read on. Her voice grew quieter.

Note by the inspector. The disciple Mok Yeon, sixteen years old, never lies and hides everything. He knows who attacked the sect and is not permitted to say. I recommend keeping an eye on him. Not as a suspect.

The Master looked up from the paper. At Mok Yeon.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quietly] As a witness.

At the very bottom of the second page was one more sentence. It wasn't part of the report. It had been written with a different brush, later, smaller.

I asked the Seoan branch who requested that I examine the complaint. They did not answer me. I am still asking. B.

***

They hung Current that evening.

On the third hook. Beside Dew. Dal guided the chain with his hands, and Do-yun pulled on the pulley, and when the new golden bell hung beside the two old green ones, Dal stepped back and sat down on the top rung of the ladder and said nothing more.

Three bells. Six empty hooks.

Mok Yeon laid his hand on Dawn. Seol-a laid hers on Dew. And Chil, whom no one had asked, laid his on Current.

They listened until all three came to rest.

Then they pulled.

Mok Yeon had believed he knew what two bells were. A third tone out of the air between them. He hadn't known what three bells were. They weren't three tones and three more between. They were a chord. A sound that built itself up like a house, story upon story, in which every tone had its place and none stood alone. It went through the tower and over the courtyard and over the mountain, and down in Hacheon, people said later, the dogs stopped and raised their heads.

And Mok Yeon heard, beneath the chord, very quietly, very far down, the three hearts beside him.

And beneath the hearts he heard what they didn't say.

Chil, thinking: I called a bell.

Seol-a, thinking: He should have heard this.

And himself, for the first time in forty-one years, with no lie beneath.
