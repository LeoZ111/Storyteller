# Chapter 19: Lantern Night

For the last sixteen days, no one on Unmu spoke loudly.

It hadn't been agreed. It simply happened. Do-yun carried the last stones for the wall, and he no longer hummed while he did it. Dal cooked, and he didn't clatter the pots. Seol-a practiced on the ground, with no tassel on her sword, and sometimes she stopped in the middle of the fourth step and listened south, north, in every direction, with the new expression she had brought back from Seoan. As if she could no longer switch the world off.

She had told them everything. On the first night, in the tower, beneath the bells. The laugh. The cellar. The thank you. The ancestral hall. And then she had looked at Mok Yeon for a long time and said: Now you. And he had told her. The whole story, for the third time in his life, and the third time he told it better.

She hadn't cried. She had only nodded once, at the very end, when he told her about the rumors of the Frost Blossom Blade. And then she said:

[Yu Seol-a|quiet, hard] Not this time.

***

They didn't flee.

They had talked about it. For a long time. The Master had proposed it one last time, in the kitchen, at night, with all five of them at the table. If they left now, in every direction, the lanterns would find an empty mountain on Mid-Autumn night.

[Mok Yeon|calm] And then they hunt us. One by one. In a month, in a year. He knows now that we know. He won't stop until we're silent.

[Han Do-yun|slowly] And the bells stay here.

[Dal|growling, from the stove] And my stove.

The Master looked at them all, one after another. Then she took the pipe out of her mouth.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] Then we stay. And we make sure all of Hacheon looks up.

That was the plan. It was simple, and it was all they had.

The lanterns were coming because they believed no one would look up on Mid-Autumn night. All of Hacheon would be at the river, lanterns in hand, with music and rice wine and fireworks. Five people on a mountain couldn't win against twenty murderers. But they could hold out. And they could ring.

The next day Mok Yeon went to Madam Ok.

He didn't tell her everything. He told her enough. That men would come on Mid-Autumn night. That the bells of Unmu would ring then, not in the rhythm of prayer but differently, fast, Dawn and Dew alternating, over and over. And he asked her to send people up when that happened. Her porters. The town watch. Whoever she could get.

Madam Ok cracked a seed. Ping. She looked at him for a long time with her small black bird's eyes.

[Madam Ok|quiet, without mockery] Who's sending the men?

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] I can't tell you that.

[Madam Ok|nodding] Then it's someone whose name one doesn't say.

She cracked another seed. This time the shell didn't fly into the brass dish. It landed on the counter, and Madam Ok looked at it as if it had just told her something important.

[Madam Ok|curt] Twenty porters. And the captain of the town watch owes me for a ring he never collected. When your bells scream, we'll come. And boy.

He waited.

[Madam Ok|quieter] Send me the child. The round one. For the night. He can see the Lantern Festival here. Children should see the Lantern Festival.

***

Chil refused.

He refused in every way available to a thirteen-year-old. He shouted. He cried. He hid in the woodpile, in the furnace behind the tower, in the empty chest Dusk's beam had come in. He said he was a guard. He had the Sparrow Step. He had hung the deepest bell at the side gate. If thieves came, he had to be there.

They hadn't told him who was coming. The Master had decided. He was thirteen. He had a heart like an open window. If he knew, he'd tell someone at the tofu stall by the next day at the latest.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|hard] You're going to Madam Ok's. You'll watch the Lantern Festival. You'll eat mooncakes until you're sick. And you'll come back up tomorrow morning.

[Bang Chil|crying, furious] You don't want me here! You never want me here! Seol-a got to go to Seoan, and Do-yun gets to get married, and Mok Yeon gets to be a ghost, and I have to eat mooncakes!

He turned and ran out, and this time you could hear every step.

Mok Yeon found him that evening on the wall beside the main gate. He was sitting with his back to the courtyard, looking down into the valley.

Mok Yeon sat beside him. For a long time neither said anything.

[Bang Chil|finally, quietly, sniffling] You are a ghost. Ghosts know things. Tell me what's going to happen.

Mok Yeon looked at him. The round face. The mole under his left eye. Eyes red from crying. He thought of a single brief sound.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] I want you to live, Chil. That's all I know.

Chil looked at him. He didn't understand. But he heard something in the voice he had never heard before, and he stopped crying.

[Bang Chil|whispering] Then I'll go. But only because you're a ghost.

***

On the afternoon of the fifteenth, Do-yun took him down.

Mok Yeon stood in the tower and watched them go. The First Disciple's broad back, and beside it the small round figure that turned around twice and then not again. Down at the road, at the bend where the steps ended, they stopped, and Do-yun bent down and said something, and Chil nodded. Then they walked on, toward Hacheon, where the first lanterns were already hanging along the bank, red and yellow and white, and where you could hear drums from far off.

He watched them until he couldn't see them anymore. Then he closed his eyes and breathed.

Nine and one.

In sixteen days his body had grown more than in the sixty before. He didn't know exactly why. Maybe the furnace. Every evening he had stood beside Dal while he cast, and heard the tone of the bronze as it came out of the mold, and every time something in his meridians had answered. Maybe the sound net. Every night he had sat in the tower listening to the forty-seven little bells, and his hearing had widened a little more each night, all on its own, without force.

Three days ago, during morning stance, the door had opened and not swung shut again. Bell Ear. The Third Tone. Not as memory. In the flesh.

It cost nothing anymore. No blood. Only the whine in his left ear, which never quite left him.

He heard the mountain. He heard Dal in the kitchen sharpening a cleaver, slowly, with great care. He heard Seol-a at the west wall drawing her sword and sheathing it, drawing and sheathing. He heard the Master at the main gate, her breathing calm, the crackle of the pipe. He heard Chestnut in the stable, restlessly pawing the ground.

And far down the goat path, where it reached the stream, he heard a single little bell. Very faint. Not the wind.

Someone was standing down there. Waiting. Like before.

***

Do-yun came back as the sun went down.

He came alone. He said Chil was at Madam Ok's. He said the boy hadn't looked at him when they said goodbye. He said Min-ji was down at the riverbank with her father, holding a lantern shaped like a rabbit, and she had slapped him when he told her he had to go up the mountain tonight, and then she had kissed him in front of everyone.

He didn't tell it well. He left out the most important part, as always. But he stood at the main gate beside the Master as he told it, holding the bad, blunt sword that had once broken a saber into four pieces, and he didn't look like someone who was afraid.

He looked like a wall.

***

The moon rose. Full and yellow and enormous, above the eastern hills.

Down in Hacheon a thousand lanterns were burning. Mok Yeon saw them from the tower, a river of light along the bank of the real river, and on the water floated small paper boats with candles in them, hundreds, drifting slowly downstream. You could hear the music. Flutes and drums and sometimes a laugh that drifted all the way up the mountain.

He remembered this. In his first life he had seen exactly this view from the tower before he curled up inside the bell. The lanterns along the river. The boats. He had thought how beautiful it was. He had thought, tomorrow I'll go down and buy a mooncake.

Then the other lanterns had come.

He laid his hand on Dawn. He closed his eyes.

The night grew deeper.

***

It was just after midnight when the sound net began to sing.

First the goat path. Down by the stream, the path's deepest bell. Then the next, higher. Then two in quick succession, by the rocks. Not hurried. Not like a traveler. Steady, step by step, as if the people walking there knew perfectly well they were touching bells and didn't care.

Mok Yeon counted. He counted the gaps between the notes, the pauses, the overlaps.

Eight. On the goat path. Toward the west wall.

Then the main path. The bells in the trees where Do-yun had hung them. More. Many more. Twelve. Fourteen. They were coming up the road quite openly, and at the foot of the steps, at the bend, all the bells sang at once, and then it stopped.

They were standing at the bottom. Waiting.

He struck Dawn. Once. The agreed signal. Main gate.

He struck Dew. Twice. West wall.

Down in the courtyard he heard the Master stand. He heard Do-yun's sword slide from its scabbard, heavy and slow. He heard Seol-a at the wall holding her breath, nine and one, and then the moment after.

Then he heard something that wasn't part of the plan.

Behind the kitchen. At the side gate.

The deepest little bell. The one Chil had hung. It rang. Once. Softly. As if someone outside had nudged the thread very carefully to check it was there.

The side gate. The new bolt. Only from inside. It was safe. No one was getting through from outside.

He listened anyway. He dropped Bell Ear like a stone into a pond, fully, with everything he had now, and the world opened.

He heard Dal in the kitchen, cleaver in hand, his heart slow and old.

And he heard, in the woodpile beside the kitchen, between the split logs, under a tarp, a second heart. Small. Fast. A little too fast, because it was excited.

Mok Yeon froze.

He hadn't heard it. Not all evening. He had listened across the mountain, every stone, every tree, and he hadn't heard this heart, because he hadn't been looking for it. Because it was in Hacheon, at Madam Ok's, with mooncakes. Because it knew the Sparrow Step. Because it knew exactly where every little bell on the main path hung, because it had helped hang them, and could step over every thread without touching it.

Chil.

The deepest bell rang again. Outside, at the side gate.

And in the woodpile the small heart moved. It came out. Silently. Sparrow Step. Outer edge, ball, the heel a guest. It went to the side gate. It was excited. It was proud. Mok Yeon heard it as clearly as if he stood beside it: the bell is ringing, so a thief is caught in the thread, and I'm the guard, and I'll catch him.

[Mok Yeon|roaring, from the tower] CHIL! DON'T!

His voice rolled across the courtyard. It came too late. It came one heartbeat too late, the way it had come forty-one years too late in his first life.

He heard a small hand turn the handle of the new bolt. And pull.

And he heard wood creak.

At the back. By the kitchen.

The same creak. Exactly the same. The creak he hadn't been able to place for forty-one years, that he had heard in caves and on peaks and in prisons, every night before he fell asleep. A side gate being opened from inside. By a small boy who wanted to sneak off to the Lantern Festival. Or by a small boy who wanted to catch a thief.

It had always been Chil.

***

Three men came through the open side gate.

Mok Yeon heard them. He heard Chil's heart stop in fright and then race on. He heard a man breathing through his nose with a faint whistle, as if it had once been broken. He heard a brass buckle. Tick.

He heard the man with the broken nose seize Chil by the collar and lift him up, the way you lift a cat.

And then he heard the man's voice, quiet and matter-of-fact and a little tired.

[Lantern Man|quiet, matter-of-fact] A child. Another child.

He let Chil go. He shoved him away, to the side, into the courtyard, not hard.

[Lantern Man|curt] Run. We don't kill children. Run away, boy.

Chil ran. Mok Yeon heard him run across the courtyard, not silently, every step, and heard where he was running. Not to the gate. Not into the kitchen. To the tower. To him.

And then everything broke loose.

***

At the main gate, Do-yun roared.

It was the same roar. Deep and long, like an ox. Mok Yeon heard it, and for one heartbeat he was back in the bronze and the lanterns were burning outside. He heard steel on steel, the crash as someone charged the gate, wood splintering.

But then he heard the roar go on. And on. And beneath it, in the middle of the din, a sound that hadn't existed in his first life: a bad, blunt sword ringing like a bell struck with an open palm.

A saber shattered. Someone screamed, and it wasn't Do-yun.

At the west wall he heard Seol-a. He barely heard her, she was so fast. He heard her feet on the new stones, her sword cutting the air, and nine steps, over and over nine, and every fourth one was no longer hollow.

From the kitchen he heard Dal bellow something obscene that Mok Yeon hadn't heard in forty-one years, and then the hiss of boiling water on skin, and a man screaming.

At the main gate he heard the Master. She wasn't fighting. Not yet. She stood behind Do-yun, and he heard her voice, calm and clear, as in morning stance.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, loud] Left, Do-yun. Two breaths. Now right. Low.

And Do-yun moved, and the roar went on.

Mok Yeon stood in the tower beneath Dawn holding the rope, hearing the whole mountain fight, and for a moment he was so proud of all of them he couldn't breathe.

Then he heard the ladder.

Small, quick steps. Chil. He came up, panting, sobbing, and fell onto the boards at the top, and crawled to Mok Yeon and clung to his leg.

[Bang Chil|sobbing, beside himself] I wanted to catch the thief. I only wanted to catch the thief. The bell rang, and I thought… I thought…

Mok Yeon laid a hand on his head. He couldn't say anything.

And beneath Chil's sobbing, beneath the roar at the gate, beneath the clash at the wall, he heard the ladder again.

He didn't hear it. That was the terrible thing. He heard no footsteps. He only heard the wood of the ladder bending, rung by rung, under a weight that made no sound. And he smelled ink.

A head appeared over the edge of the tower floor. A friendly, clean-shaven face that recalled a village schoolteacher. Then shoulders in grey. Then the whole man, stepping lightly onto the boards and stopping there, between the two bells and the boy clinging to Mok Yeon's leg.

Hwang Ryeom had his hands in his sleeves. He looked around. The bells. The seven empty hooks. The rope in Mok Yeon's hand.

He smiled. Politely. A little sadly.

[Hwang Ryeom|quiet, friendly] Good evening. A lovely evening for lanterns.

Mok Yeon looked at him. He saw the bell called Dawn beside him, big enough for a skinny boy to disappear into, if he made himself small enough. He had made himself very small once.

He stepped in front of Chil.

He tightened his fingers around the rope.
