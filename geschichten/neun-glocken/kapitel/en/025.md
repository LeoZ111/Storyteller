# Chapter 25: The River Bell

On the eighth day of the tenth month, half of Hacheon stood at Bell Bend.

Nobody had invited them. Madam Ok had sent twenty porters and two flat barges that usually carried rice across the river, and word got around. Then Old Gong had told the harbor teahouse that the bell in the river had sung again after sixty years, and that got around even faster. And then someone heard that the Murim Alliance's inspector would be there to decide whether the people from Unmu were demons, and nobody wanted to miss that.

They stood on the bank between the willows, on the rocks, on overturned boats. Fishermen and porters and market women with baskets of dumplings they sold on the spot. The old woman with the radish cart. The farmhand from Pyo's agency, who now bowed to Mok Yeon at every opportunity. Children climbing trees to see better.

And on a folding stool her clerk had brought, at the edge of the crowd, the wooden box on her knees: Baek Ha-seon.

***

[Bang Chil|whispering, overwhelmed] There's a hundred people.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Two hundred and twelve.

Chil looked at him.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] I can hear them. Every heart is a little different.

He hadn't counted. He had simply known when he arrived at the bend, the way you know how many fingers you have. Since Mid-Autumn night, Bell Ear was no longer a door he had to push open. It stood open. Always. Even through the hum in his left ear, which no longer went away. He heard the crowd on the bank like a forest in the wind, and beneath it the river, and beneath that, very deep, something sleeping.

Current.

***

Do-yun had been building for three days.

On the rock that jutted into the bend like a nose there now stood a frame of three young pine trunks, lashed together at the top like a giant tripod. From it hung the pulley he had built from a wagon wheel for Dew, reinforced with a second wheel and ropes from Madam Ok's warehouse as thick as a child's arm. The rope ran from the pulley over the rock to the bank, where it was looped around a willow trunk, and from there on to a leather harness.

In the harness stood the tofu seller's ox.

It stood there and looked at the river. Then at the people. Then at the rope. And then it lay down.

[Min-ji|desperate, at the ox's head] Get up! It's a flat path! There aren't any steps! Get up!

The ox closed its eyes.

A murmur went through the crowd. Someone laughed. Master Pyeon, standing at the edge with four of his disciples, laughed loudest.

Chil saw that. He turned, ran to the old woman's cart, grabbed two radishes, called a thank you over his shoulder that she answered with a toothless grin, and ran back. He stood two paces in front of the ox. He held up the radishes.

The ox opened one eye.

[Bang Chil|coaxing] Come on. Come on, big fellow. Nice radishes. Very fresh.

The ox opened the other eye. It thought. It took a long time. Then it got up, slowly, like an old man getting out of a chair, and took a step toward the radishes.

The crowd cheered. Chil turned red to his ears and held the radishes higher.

At the edge, on her folding stool, Baek Ha-seon noted something down. Her clerk, reading over her shoulder, had to cough to keep from laughing.

***

But first they had to find the bell.

Old Gong sat on his rock as always, with his hookless rod, and pointed at the water with a bony finger.

[Old Gong|cracked, certain] There. Where the water turns. Two men deep. Maybe three. It's lying on its side. I saw it fall.

But sixty years is a long time, and a river moves mud. The fisher boys Madam Ok had paid dove all morning. They came up with mud in their hair and weeds on their arms and said it was so dark down there you couldn't see your own hand. They had found stones, an old oar, the skull of a cow. No bell.

By midday the sun was high and the crowd was getting restless. Master Pyeon said loudly that the bell was a fairy tale a blind man had made up. A few people nodded.

Mok Yeon took off his shoes.

[Han Do-yun|alarmed] Your hand.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I'm not diving. I'm listening.

He waded into the river. From the rock, where it deepened gradually. The water was cold, autumn cold, and the current tugged at his legs. Knee-deep. Waist-deep. Chest-deep. He held his bandaged hand above the water. Then he stopped.

[Mok Yeon|loud, to the bank] Chil. The little bell.

Chil understood at once. He had brought it on a cord around his neck, the way Seol-a wore the one from Storm: one of the forty-seven little bells from the sound net, the middle one, which had hung on the goat path by the rocks. He pushed the radishes into Min-ji's hands, climbed onto the rock beside Old Gong, held the little bell out over the water, and flicked it with his fingernail.

A bright note. Small. Like a drop.

Mok Yeon closed his eyes.

He heard the note run across the water. He heard it strike the surface and sink in, a tiny bit, the way light sinks into water. And far, far below, he heard something large stir. Like someone hearing their name in their sleep.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Again.

Chil struck the bell again. And again. And again. One small bright note after another, and the crowd on the bank went quiet without knowing why, and listened.

Mok Yeon took a step to the left. The water rose to his neck. He heard the hum. It was faint. He took a step back. And one forward, against the current, his feet in the mud.

Here. The hum was stronger here. He felt it in the soles of his feet, through the mud, the way he had felt it with his hand on the rock.

He stretched out his arm and pointed at a spot in the water an arm's length ahead of him, where the surface was smooth and dark.

[Mok Yeon|calm, loud] Here. Directly below me. It isn't lying on its side. It's standing. Crown up. Half in the mud.

And on the surface, exactly where he was pointing, a ring formed. No wind. No fish. It spread slowly, silver in the noon light.

The crowd on the bank held its breath.

***

Seol-a dove.

She had insisted. Mok Yeon couldn't with his hand, Do-yun couldn't with his shoulder, and the fisher boys didn't know what to grab. Seol-a had been born in a warehouse by the river and could swim before she could walk.

She took off her outer robe, tied her hair up with the white cord, and took the end of the thick rope between her teeth. Then she waded out to Mok Yeon, breathed in nine times, each time deeper, and on the tenth she vanished beneath the surface.

Mok Yeon heard her heart. It sank with her. Slowly. Calmly. Nine and one.

He counted. Ten heartbeats. Twenty. Thirty.

On the bank, Min-ji stood with the radishes and the ox, her hand over her mouth. The Master stood on the rock beside Old Gong, head tilted, her left ear toward the water.

Forty.

Down below in the dark, Mok Yeon heard Seol-a's hands feeling over bronze. He didn't hear it. He felt it, because the bell felt it, and because the bell was humming, and because he was standing in the water that carried the hum. The shoulder. The curve. At the very top the crown, the bronze ring a bell hangs from, half buried in mud. He heard her fingers scoop the mud away. Heard the rope slide through the ring. Heard her tie a knot, one Do-yun had shown her fifty times the evening before, until she could do it with her eyes shut.

Fifty.

She came up beside him, spluttering, mud on her face and weeds in her hair, and she was grinning. He had never seen her grin like that.

[Yu Seol-a|panting] It's huge. It's bigger than Dawn. And it's humming. I felt it in my teeth.

***

Then they pulled.

The ox pulled, because Chil ran two steps ahead of it waving radishes. Do-yun pulled with his good arm, feet braced in the bank mud. Madam Ok's porters pulled. And then, as the rope went taut and creaked and nothing moved, the others came.

The fishermen. The porters. The farmhand from Pyo's agency. Min-ji's father, the tofu seller, a small fat man who had never lifted anything heavy in his life. The old woman with the radish cart, who laid her bony hands on the rope the way she had laid them on the runner when they dragged Dew up the steps, and it did just as little good, and she pulled anyway.

In the end forty people held the rope. Maybe fifty.

And at the edge of the crowd, Baek Ha-seon stood up from her folding stool. She set down the wooden box. She walked to the rope, took it in both hands between a fisherman and a porter, and pulled.

Her clerk stared at her. Then he put his paper on the stool and pulled as well.

Guard Tak said nothing. He went to the end of the rope, wound it twice around his forearms, and pulled until the veins stood out on his neck.

Master Pyeon stood at the edge with his four disciples and watched. One of them, the young one who held his reins like a broom, looked at his master. Then he went to the rope.

***

The bell came slowly.

At first Mok Yeon only heard it, because he was standing in the water. A sucking. The mud letting go after sixty years, reluctantly, with a deep gurgling sound that ran through the water like a sigh. Then the surface moved. Bubbles rose, brown and large. Then a shadow beneath the water, dark and round.

Then the crown broke the surface.

Green. Not Dawn's dull green, not Dew's blotchy green. A deep, gleaming, almost black green, hung with weeds like hair, water streaming from the bronze. The shoulder came up. The inscription at the rim, overgrown, illegible. The rope creaked. The pulley groaned. Do-yun roared, deep and long like an ox, and the ox roared back.

Current hung above the water. Enormous. Dripping. It turned slowly on the rope, water pouring out of it as from an upended jug, and the noon sun gleamed on the wet bronze.

The crowd on the bank screamed. Cheered. Clapped. Children fell out of trees with excitement.

And then the bell turned further, and Mok Yeon saw the other side.

A crack. From the rim upward, slanting, a hand's width long, then two, then almost to the shoulder. Black and jagged, like lightning in the bronze. Where the bell had struck the bottom sixty years ago.

The bell swung on the rope. A piece of driftwood the river pushed along knocked against its rim.

It rang.

And it was a terrible sound. Not a tone. A clatter, rough and splintered, like broken porcelain in a sack. It ran over the water and over the bank and over the two hundred and twelve people, and the cheering broke off as if someone had laid a hand on it.

It was very quiet at Bell Bend.

Mok Yeon stood chest-deep in the water and looked at the bell. He heard the crack. He heard it run through the tone like a scar through a face.

On the rock stood Old Gong. He had dropped his rod. He held his bony hands out in front of him as if to catch something, and his face with its milky eyes was wet.

[Old Gong|cracked, weeping] It's alive. It's only hurt. Sixty years. It's only hurt.

***

They loaded the bell onto one of the barges. It took them two hours. By the time they finished the sun was already low, and most people went home, wet and muddy and strangely quiet. Some bowed to the Master before they left. Some said nothing.

Dal stood on the bank beside the barge and hadn't said anything the whole time. Now he climbed up, on his bad knees, and laid his scarred hands on the crack. He ran them along it, from the rim almost to the shoulder, slowly, the way a physician runs his fingers along a wound.

[Dal|quiet, rough] Nobody can close that. No smith. No solderer. A cracked bell stays cracked.

The Master stood on the bank. She said nothing.

[Dal|after a long pause, without taking his hands away] I'll recast it.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|too loud] What?

[Dal|louder, growling] I'll recast it. Out of itself. I'll melt it down, the way I melted Storm and Snow. But this time I'll cast it again. In the same shape. With the same voice. I know how it has to sound. I heard it when I was a boy. Before it fell.

He took his hands off the bronze and looked at them. The scars gleamed in the evening sun.

[Dal|quietly] For thirty years I thought these hands could only break things.

***

Baek Ha-seon came to the Master as the barges cast off.

She was wet to the knees. There was mud from the rope on her hands. Her clerk carried the wooden box and the folding stool and looked as if he'd had the best day of his life.

The inspector stood to the Master's left so she would hear her.

[Baek Ha-seon|matter-of-fact] Today I noted the following. The sect located a sunken bell by sound. No one's qi was stolen, poisoned, or destroyed in the process. Around two hundred residents of Hacheon helped voluntarily, including a disciple of the complaining school.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] And you.

[Baek Ha-seon|without embarrassment] And me. I didn't note that.

She looked at the barge where Current lay, wet and green and cracked.

[Baek Ha-seon|quietly] I've been an inspector for twelve years, Sect Leader. I've dissolved three demonic sects. Not one of them got half a town to pull it out of a river.

She bowed. Exactly as deeply as always. And left.

Mok Yeon was still standing in the water, knee-deep now, watching her go. Behind him on the rock, Old Gong sat again with his hookless rod, his face turned to the evening sun, smiling. Beside him sat Chil, the little bell still around his neck, radish leaves in his hair.

[Bang Chil|quietly] Grandfather Gong. Now that it's gone. What are you fishing for now?

Old Gong thought about it for a long time.

[Old Gong|cracked, content] Nothing anymore. I just listen.
