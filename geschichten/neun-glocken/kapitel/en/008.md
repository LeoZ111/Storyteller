# Chapter 8: Fire on the River

On the night of the third day, Mok Yeon went down the mountain alone.

He didn't take the main path with its forty-seven steps. He climbed over the collapsed west wall, through the thornbrush, and followed the goat path. The grass stood hip-high and wet with dew, and after twenty paces his trousers were soaked to the knee. He thought of a polite man in grey who came back to an inn every night with wet boots. He thought about how he was now walking the same path.

The moon was nearly full. Hacheon lay below him like a handful of embers in a bowl: a few lights at the harbor, a few in the teahouses, the river a black ribbon with silver scales.

He hadn't told anyone. He knew it was wrong. The Master had forbidden him to listen alone at night, and he had promised not to. He didn't break that promise lightly. But for forty-one years he had believed the men with the lanterns came out of nowhere, and tonight some of them would set something on fire, and he wanted to see who.

He wanted to see their faces. Just once. Before they came up the mountain.

***

The row of warehouses on the river lay silent.

Twelve buildings of timber and clay, wall to wall, with grey tile roofs that huddled together in the moonlight like sleeping animals. At one end the great granary of the Yun family, with a carved dragon above its gate. At the other the ropewalk, a long, low shed that smelled of hemp and tar. Behind it, between the ropewalk and the river wall, stood barrels. Ten, twelve, stacked in two rows and tied down with rope.

Mok Yeon crouched in the shadow of an overturned boat on the bank, thirty paces away, and waited.

He heard the river. He heard the wooden piers creak whenever a wave ran beneath them. He heard a dog in the harbor whimpering in its sleep. Somewhere behind the warehouses he heard two night watchmen laughing, men who had drunk too much rice wine.

He didn't hear the men arrive.

That was the first thing that frightened him. They were simply there, among the barrels, two figures in dark, close-fitting clothes, as if they had grown out of the ropewalk's shadow. They didn't move like thieves, hunched and hurried. They moved like craftsmen doing a job they had done many times. One held a knife and cut the ropes that tied down the barrels. The other pulled out a bung, and Mok Yeon smelled it at once, even at that distance: lamp oil, heavy and sweetish.

At the second man's belt hung a lantern. Small, made of black paper. Unlit.

Mok Yeon closed his eyes.

He did it carefully. One drop. A single drop from the spoon, no more.

The world opened, and he heard their hearts. Calm. Far too calm. Like the hearts of men chopping wood. One of them carried something hard at his hip that tapped softly against a brass buckle with every step, tick, tick. The other breathed through his nose, evenly, with a faint whistle, as if it had once been broken.

Then the world closed again, and something throbbed behind his eyes.

The oil ran out of the barrels over the stones, into the gaps beneath the ropewalk, into the straw lying there. One of the men knelt. A flint scraped. A spark.

The fire didn't come slowly. It came like an animal that had waited a long time. A blue tongue ran across the oil, turned yellow, turned orange, leapt into the straw and from there to the ropewalk wall where the hemp ropes hung, and the ropes caught like tinder, one after another, and within a few heartbeats the entire back wall was in flames.

The light struck Mok Yeon in the face. He felt the heat across thirty paces.

The two men stood a moment longer and watched, the way you watch a pot to see if it's boiling. Then they turned and left. Not running. Walking. In two different directions.

Mok Yeon followed the one with the broken nose.

***

Behind him, Hacheon woke.

He heard the harbor bell, a small tin fire bell that someone was beating wildly. He heard shouts, doors, the clatter of buckets. He heard the roar of the fire, which had a sound of its own now, deep and hungry, and the crash as the first roof caved in. The sky above the river turned red.

He heard all of it and left it behind.

The man ahead of him walked through the alleys between the harbor and the market, through a part of town where the houses stood so close their eaves nearly touched. He turned often. He never looked back. Mok Yeon followed at forty paces, using the Sparrow Step he had taught Chil: outer edge, ball, the heel a guest. It was harder than it had looked on Chil. His body was too stiff, his legs too tired from the goat path.

He let the drop fall once more. Just to hear where the man was.

And heard nothing.

The heartbeat was gone. The breathing through the broken nose was gone. The alley ahead was empty, moonlit and empty, and at the end it bent left into a narrow passage between two workshops.

He heard it one second too late.

Tick. A brass buckle. Directly above him.

Mok Yeon threw himself sideways, and the knife that had been aimed at his neck slit his sleeve instead, from elbow to shoulder. He rolled across the cobbles and came up against a house wall, and the man landed in front of him, lightly, from a crouch, off the workshop awning where he had been waiting.

Up close he was younger than Mok Yeon had thought. Maybe thirty. A narrow, ordinary face you'd forget immediately. A nose that had healed crooked. Eyes with no expression at all.

[Lantern Man|quiet, matter-of-fact] A child. They sent a child after me.

[Mok Yeon|breathing hard] No one sent me.

[Lantern Man|nodding, uninterested] Then it's even stupider.

He attacked.

It wasn't a beautiful attack. No name, no form, nothing you would find in a book on martial arts. A knife coming from below, fast and flat, toward the liver. A man of the second rank, maybe a little above it, who had learned to kill people, not to defeat them.

Mok Yeon heard it coming. Not with Bell Ear. With what remains after forty years when everything else is gone: knowing where a knife wants to go before it gets there. He twisted his hip. The blade grazed his shirt. The second thrust came higher, toward the throat. He ducked. The third…

His body was too slow. He knew where he had to move and didn't get there in time. The knife cut across his ribs, not deep but hot, and he stumbled backward into the passage between the workshops.

Something struck the back of his head. Something cold and flat that swayed and gave off a hollow, quivering sound.

Tin.

He knew where he was. He had heard it in the market by day, the tireless hammering on sheet tin in a rhythm that matched nothing else. The tinsmith's workshop. And here, in the passage behind it, the finished sheets hung cooling on hooks from a beam, dozens of them, large flat panels of thin iron sheet, close together, like laundry on a line.

The man with the broken nose came into the passage. Slowly now. He knew his prey was trapped.

Mok Yeon put his back to the largest sheet.

He breathed. Nine times in, each deeper, letting his heartbeat sink with the breath. He felt the stream in his lower belly, warm and narrow, and felt it gather, like water behind a dam.

Then he held his breath. The tenth beat. The pause in which nothing happens. The bell that has been struck and has not yet sounded.

The man raised the knife.

Mok Yeon turned and struck the sheet with the flat of his hand.

He put everything into it. The whole Second Tone, the held breath, the vibration of his own heart. It wasn't much. In another place, under open sky, it would have been a loud slap and nothing more.

But this was a passage narrow as a chimney, with stone walls. And from the beam hung thirty sheets, so close together that each one touched the next.

The first sheet screamed. The second answered. The third, the fourth. The sound ran along the beam the way the fire had run along the ropewalk, sheet after sheet, each tuned a little differently, bouncing off the stone walls and piling onto itself, and for an instant the whole passage was one single, enormous, shrieking roar.

Mok Yeon was ready for it. He had held his breath, he had known what was coming, and still it felt as if someone had driven two needles into his ears.

The man with the broken nose was not ready.

He reeled. He clapped his free hand to his ear. And despite everything, despite the roar and the needles, Mok Yeon heard his qi stumble. It was as if someone had played a wrong note in the middle of a song, so loud the singer lost his place. The flow in his meridians faltered for the span of one heartbeat, and for that heartbeat he was just a man with a knife who didn't know which way was up.

Mok Yeon stepped in and struck the inside of his right wrist with the edge of his hand. Right on the tendon. Just like with Seol-a, only with everything he had.

The knife clattered onto the cobbles.

The man looked at him. For the first time there was something in his eyes. Not fear. Something colder. Interest.

Then shouts came from the end of the alley. Lanterns, ordinary yellow lanterns. People who had heard the roar and were coming to look, buckets still in their hands.

The man stepped back. He didn't stoop for the knife. He sprang up the wall onto the awning and was gone, as silently as he had come.

Mok Yeon stood in the passage between the slowly swaying sheets and couldn't stop shaking.

On the cobbles beside the knife lay something that had torn loose from the man's belt when he leapt. A small charm, no bigger than a thumb: a lantern of black lacquer on a silk cord, with three fine white lines on its side.

And beside it, burst open, a leather pouch. A folded paper stuck out of it.

***

Han Do-yun found him half an hour later at the well behind the market.

Mok Yeon sat on the rim of the well, the slit sleeve wrapped around his ribs, and blood ran from both his ears down his neck in two thin dark lines that gleamed in the firelight. The whole sky over the river was red. The row of warehouses was burning from the Yun family granary to the ropewalk, just like in his first life, and the fire was reflected in the water so that the river seemed to be burning too.

Do-yun stood in front of him, breathing hard, shirt buttoned wrong, barefoot. He hadn't even put on shoes.

[Han Do-yun|between anger and relief] I woke up and your heart was gone. I used Bell Ear, I listened to the whole mountain, and your heart wasn't there. I thought…

He didn't finish. He saw the blood.

[Mok Yeon|hoarse, barely audible] It burned.

[Han Do-yun|quietly] I can see that.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Madam Ok's warehouse is empty. She cleared it. Like I said.

Do-yun understood none of it. He asked no questions. He knelt in front of the well, turned his broad back to Mok Yeon, and waited.

[Han Do-yun|grumbling] Climb on.

Mok Yeon climbed on. The First Disciple's back was warm and smelled of sleep. He put his arms around Do-yun's neck, and in his fist he held the black charm and the folded paper, and he didn't let go of either, not even when his eyes fell shut halfway up the mountain.

Before they closed, he looked back once more.

Down on the river, in the middle of the fire's red reflection, a small boat was drifting. A man stood in it. He wore grey. He had his hands tucked into his sleeves and was watching the warehouses burn, calmly, the way you watch a pot to see if it's boiling.

And then he turned his head. Toward the bank. Toward the mountain path. Toward a broad man carrying a boy on his back.

He raised a hand. Politely. As if in greeting.

***

It was already light when Mok Yeon unfolded the paper.

He sat on his straw mat. Dal had bandaged his ribs without saying a single word, which was worse than any scolding. Outside he heard the Master crossing the courtyard with footsteps he had never heard from her before, hard, fast footsteps. She would come in any moment. He didn't have much time.

The paper was thin and cheap. On it, in black ink, someone had made a drawing, clean and precise, in a practiced hand.

It was Unmu.

The mountain, the path, the forty-seven steps. The courtyard, the main hall, the kitchen, the tower. The collapsed west wall, marked with a small cross. The goat path as a dotted line. Beside the tower a tiny note: bell, one. Beside the kitchen, a circle.

The side gate.

And next to it, in small, neat handwriting:

Bolted inside. Bolt weak. Wait.
