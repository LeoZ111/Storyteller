# Chapter 16: Farewell

The west camp lay outside the gates of Hacheon on a field where millet used to grow.

Nothing grew there now. Three thousand people had trampled the earth until it was hard as a threshing floor, and on it stood tents of patched tarps, huts of reed and mud, shelters made of nothing but a plank and a blanket. Smoke hung over everything. It smelled of charcoal and latrines and boiled barley. Children ran between the tents barefoot, with swollen bellies and thin arms, playing a game where they threw stones to one another.

Mok Yeon stood at the edge and watched.

It was the fifth day of their protection contract. Every other morning two of them walked the road between the west gate and the camp, an hour there, an hour back, escorting the carts that brought rice. The Master had assigned the pairs. Do-yun with Seol-a. Mok Yeon with Do-yun. Chil wasn't allowed because he was too young, and hadn't spoken to her for three days.

Today Mok Yeon was paired with Seol-a.

She stood beside him, also watching the sacks being lifted from the cart. Each sack had a sign painted on it in red. The blue sword over the wave. And beside it, smaller, the name of the Yun family.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Eight hundred sacks.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Yes.

[Yu Seol-a|after a pause] Ui-jin says his father didn't sleep for three nights when he heard about the children in the camp. He says his father barely sleeps anyway. He's in pain. Ever since the pass.

Mok Yeon said nothing.

A small boy came over to them. Maybe six years old, with a head that looked too big for his thin neck and a blade of grass in the corner of his mouth. He looked up at them. At their swords. Then he took the grass out of his mouth and held it out to Seol-a.

Seol-a stared at him.

Then she knelt and took the blade of grass and thanked him, seriously, as if for a great gift, and the boy grinned and ran off.

She stayed on her knees. The grass in her hand.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible] That's the boy from the speech.

Mok Yeon watched the child go. He thought of a storeroom he had never seen, a second heartbeat, people screaming behind a thick door. And of eight hundred sacks of rice with a red sign, and a boy who would eat barley tonight instead of grass.

Both were true. That was the worst of it. It had taken him forty-one years to understand that both could be true.

[Yu Seol-a|standing, the blade of grass in her sleeve] He's a good person. The father too. I don't know why you look at him like that, Mok Yeon. At the gathering. And when Ui-jin talked about him. You look at him as if he took something from you.

He looked at her. He wanted to tell her. It sat on his tongue, heavy as bronze.

He didn't.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Listen when you're there. That's all.

***

The weeks went faster than he wanted.

In the mornings he stood with the Master and Do-yun on the training ground. In the late morning he scrubbed the latrine, because the Master insisted and because from the latrine yard you could see the west wall and the goat path. In the afternoons he walked the road to the camp with Do-yun or Seol-a. In the evenings he sat beneath the bells and counted.

His body grew. Slowly. Too slowly. The stream in his belly was a narrow river now, and on some mornings, when the air was damp and he had slept well, he felt Bell Ear at the edge of his awareness during stance training, entirely on its own, like a door opening a crack without anyone pushing it. The whine in his left ear had grown quieter. It wasn't gone.

Fifty-two days. Fifty. Forty-seven.

The camphor beam leaned against the wall. No one touched it. Dal walked past it every morning without looking at it, the way you walk past a grave you don't want to visit.

***

On the last evening before Seol-a's departure, Dal came out of the kitchen and called her over.

Mok Yeon was sitting on the wall where Chil usually sat, and he saw it. The old man stood in the kitchen doorway, small and bent, holding something in his scarred fist. Seol-a went to him. She had to stoop, he was so small.

Dal opened his hand.

In it lay a bell. A tiny bell, no bigger than a walnut, on a cord of red silk thread. The bronze was dark, almost black, and on one side it had a rough patch where the metal hadn't come out quite smooth.

[Dal|growling, not looking at her] This is from Storm.

Seol-a froze.

[Dal|quiet, rough] A piece of it. When I melted it down, a piece broke off the rim before it was fully liquid. I kept it. Thirty years I had it in a tin under the stove. I never knew what for. Last week I knew.

He held out the little bell. She didn't take it.

[Dal|grumbling] Take it. So you can hear where you come from. In Seoan they have bigger bells. Prettier ones. But this one's ours.

Seol-a took it. Her hand was shaking. She held the small dark bell up by its red cord, and it turned in the firelight from the stove, and as it turned it gave off a note, very high, very soft, like a drop on a stone.

Up in the tower, Dew hummed along. Just for a moment. Mok Yeon heard it. Seol-a heard it too; he saw it in her face.

She said nothing. She bowed to Dal, deeper than she had ever bowed to anyone on this mountain, deeper than to the Master, and Dal waved her off, grumpy, and went back to his stove and clattered the lid and hummed his tune with no beginning and no end.

***

Mok Yeon found her on the training ground that night.

He had known she would be there. She was practicing. Not the Bell Strike Sword. She was simply standing there, eyes closed, in the middle of the ground, breathing. The little bell hung at her throat, under her robe.

[Yu Seol-a|without opening her eyes] You're sneaking around again.

[Mok Yeon|stopping at the edge] Yes.

[Yu Seol-a|calm] I'm trying to hear what you said. Beneath the voices. It doesn't work. I hear heartbeats. I hear when someone's afraid or angry. But not when they're lying. It's as if there's a wall.

He came closer. Slowly. Until he stood facing her three paces away, as in their duel.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Breathe in nine times. Each time a little deeper. Let your heartbeat sink with your breath.

She opened one eye. Suspicious.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Do it.

She closed the eye again. He listened to her. She breathed, once, twice; she was good at it, much better than he had been on his first morning, her qi ran clean, and her heart sank along with it, like a stone falling slowly through clear water. Seven. Eight. Nine.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] And now hold it. Not your breath. The moment. Like a bell that's been struck and hasn't sounded yet. There's a pause before the tone comes. Stay in the pause.

She held her breath. He heard her searching. Heard her run up against something. Heard her want to give up.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] Don't push. Wait.

Her heart beat. Once. Twice. And then, on the third, the beat didn't come where it should have, but a hair's breadth later. Not because she willed it. Because she had waited.

And in that hair's breadth, in the space between two beats, something opened.

He saw it on her face. He saw her eyebrows lift, her mouth open. She heard something. He didn't know what. Maybe the wind in the pine. Maybe Chil snoring. Maybe Dal murmuring in his sleep in the kitchen. But this time she didn't only hear it. She heard beneath it.

She breathed out. Heavily. She opened her eyes.

[Yu Seol-a|whispering] Dal was dreaming. He said something in his sleep. I heard it. It wasn't what he said. It was underneath.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Nine and one. Bell Breathing. You're not at the Fourth Tone. But you're at the door.

She looked at him. For a long time. In the moonlight her face was white.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Who taught you that? This time I want the truth.

He thought about it. He thought of the Master in the tower saying: If you lie, lie better. He didn't want to lie to Seol-a. Not on the last night.

[Mok Yeon|after a long pause] An old man. He practiced alone for a very long time. He never taught it to anyone, because there was no one there. He would have liked to teach it to you.

She looked at him as if she had heard something she didn't understand. Maybe she had. She was standing at the door to the Fourth Tone, and he didn't know how much was getting through the crack.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Is he still alive?

[Mok Yeon|calm] No.

She nodded slowly. Then she turned and went to her room, and at the door she stopped, the way everyone on this mountain stopped at doors to say one more thing.

[Yu Seol-a|without turning] Thank you. For the old man.

***

The carriage came on the morning of the third day of the eighth month.

Ui-jin came himself. He dismounted, bowed to the Master, and helped the coachman tie down Seol-a's bundle, which was so small it looked almost lost on the luggage rack. One bundle and a sword with a tassel the color of dried blood.

They said goodbye in the courtyard. Do-yun hugged her, clumsily, too hard, the way Mok Yeon had hugged Chil on the first morning, and said something no one understood because he said it into her shoulder. Dal didn't come out of the kitchen. He clattered pots so loudly you could hear it at the gate.

Chil wasn't there.

The Master stood before Seol-a. For a long time she said nothing. Seol-a waited, back straight, and Mok Yeon saw that she was waiting for something. For a word. For the request the Master hadn't spoken since the evening with the chicken.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|finally, calm] Write to me. Every week. Madam Ok has a courier who goes to Seoan every fifth day. You give him the letter at the pawnshop in the north market. If you go a week without writing, I'll come and get you.

Seol-a blinked.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quieter] And Seol-a. The gate is open. Not because I'm asking you. Because it's open. Whenever you want. By day or by night.

Seol-a's face twitched. Once. Then she bowed, deeply, and when she straightened she no longer looked cold. She looked like a nine-year-old girl someone had carried out of a burning warehouse.

She turned and got into the carriage.

***

Mok Yeon rang the morning bell as the carriage went down the steps.

He hadn't planned it. He was suddenly in the tower with the rope in his hand, and he laid his palm on the bronze and listened until it came to rest, and then he pulled.

Dawn spoke. Dew answered. And between them the third tone, out of the air, which went across the courtyard and over the gate and down the forty-seven steps and the road, after the carriage.

Down on the road, at the bend where the steps ended, a small round figure suddenly stood in the middle of the way. The carriage stopped. Chil. He had a rice ball in his hand, wrapped in a cabbage leaf, and he held it through the window without saying anything, and a hand took it. Then he stepped aside, and the carriage drove on.

Chil stood at the bend until it was out of sight. Then he sat down on a stone by the roadside and stayed there.

Mok Yeon let go of the rope.

He had let her go. He'd had the choice, and he had let her go, the way she had gone in his first life, and he didn't know whether he had done the right thing. Maybe he would never know. That was the price of knowing the future: you only knew how it had been once. Not how it would be.

Forty-two days.

***

In the carriage, Seol-a sat by the window and looked back.

Ui-jin sat across from her, talking. About Seoan, about the hall, about the teachers. About his father, who was looking forward to meeting her. She listened. She heard his heart, and it was the same as his voice, open and warm. There was nothing beneath it.

She heard the bells too. For a long time. Longer than you should have been able to hear them. Dawn and Dew and the tone between, over the hills, through the jolting of the wheels.

And at her throat, under her robe, on its red cord, the little bell from Storm hummed softly along.
