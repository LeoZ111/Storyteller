# Chapter 5: If You Lie, Lie Better

The next morning, Mok Yeon didn't ring the morning bell.

Chil did. He did it with great enthusiasm and no sense of rhythm whatsoever: three strikes, then a long pause in which he had apparently forgotten how many were left, then two hurried ones after. The bell called Dawn endured it with the patience of something a hundred and forty years old. Down in the valley, the same dog as yesterday started barking.

Mok Yeon lay on his straw mat and listened. His shoulder was blue, his thigh too, and on his cheek he wore a red welt in the shape of a wooden blade. He hadn't felt this good in a long time.

When Chil was finished, Mok Yeon climbed the tower.

The bell was still swinging, an uneasy, offended hum. He laid his palm flat on the bronze, on the pale patch, and waited until the hum calmed beneath his hand. Then he sat down under it, back against a beam, and looked up at it.

[Mok Yeon|under his breath, matter-of-fact] So. We need to talk.

The bell was silent.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Fine. I'll start.

For forty-one years he had talked to this bell without having it. In caves where the echo threw his voice back. In inns where he lay staring at the ceiling at night. He had told it everything he couldn't tell anyone else, and it had never once disagreed. It was easier to talk to it when it was actually there. It was also stranger.

[Mok Yeon|slowly, counting] First. The body. I can't do anything with this body. I can hear, but I can't strike. I can dodge twice and then I sit down. When the lanterns come, I'll sit down.

He raised a second finger.

[Mok Yeon|continuing] Second. The Master. I can't tell her. She wouldn't believe me. Or she would believe me, and then she'd go to him. She would tell him to his face what she heard. That's who she is. And then maybe the lanterns come in the seventh month instead of the ninth. And I have even less time.

A third finger.

[Mok Yeon|quieter] Third. The side gate. Someone opened it that night. I don't know who. I don't know whether it was one of us.

He stayed silent with that thought for a long time. Then he raised the fourth finger.

[Mok Yeon|thoughtful] Fourth. The lanterns are already here. At the Willow Branch. On the goat path. They've been watching us for weeks, maybe months. I didn't know that. I didn't know a lot of things. I sat on mountains for forty years thinking I knew everything, because I knew how it ends.

He looked at his hand, at the four raised fingers, and then he raised his thumb too.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] Fifth. You.

The bell hung motionless.

[Mok Yeon|calm] The founder cast nine of you. Not because nine is a nice number. He wrote that one bell is a note and nine bells are a voice. I practiced for forty years without a single bell. I still reached the Eighth Tone. What could I have done with nine?

He stood and brushed the dust off his trousers.

[Mok Yeon|dry] I know you can't answer. That's all right. I talked to you for forty-one years and you never answered. We're used to it.

A noise came from the ladder. A stifled snort.

Mok Yeon looked down. On the third rung from the top crouched Chil, both hands clamped around the ladder, mouth wide open, eyes even wider.

[Bang Chil|whispering, horrified] You're talking to the bell.

[Mok Yeon|unmoved] Yes.

[Bang Chil|even quieter] And you said forty-one years.

[Mok Yeon|after brief consideration] I like to exaggerate.

[Bang Chil|voice trembling] You never exaggerate. You never say anything at all. You're a bell ghost. I knew it. I told everyone, and nobody believed me.

He slid down the ladder so fast Mok Yeon could hear his trouser legs rubbing against the rungs, and ran across the courtyard. In the middle of the courtyard he stopped, turned around, and shouted up at the tower:

[Bang Chil|bravely, voice cracking] If you're a ghost, at least you're a nice one! You hugged me! Ghosts don't hug anybody!

And he was gone.

Mok Yeon watched him go. Then he rested his forehead against the bronze, and his shoulders shook, and it took a while before he realized he was laughing.

***

The Master sent for him to come to the main hall once the sun stood above the mist.

Inside, the hall was bigger than it looked from outside, and poorer. A wide room with a wooden floor that creaked underfoot. Red pillars whose paint was peeling in long strips. At the far end an altar with the ancestral tablets of the former masters, black plaques with gold characters, and in front of them a bowl holding incense sticks. Only one was burning. They couldn't afford more. Beside the altar a wooden basin sat on the floor, right beneath the spot where the two tiles were missing. It hadn't rained in the night, but the basin was there anyway, like a guest who had forgotten to leave.

Seo Hwa-ryeong sat cross-legged before the altar with her back to the door. The pipe lay on the floor beside her.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|without turning] Sit. In front of me. Not that close. There.

He sat. She looked at him for a long time, with that patient gaze that made you completely calm and completely restless at once.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] Breathe.

[Mok Yeon|confused] Master?

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|curt] You heard me. Close your eyes and breathe the way you breathe when you practice.

He closed his eyes. He breathed.

He didn't think about it. That was the mistake, and he knew it even as he made it, but forty-one years is a long time, and a sixteen-year-old's body has no habits of its own for the old ones to fight against. He breathed in nine times, each a little deeper, and with each breath he let his heartbeat sink along with it. Then he held his breath. For a tenth beat. A pause in which nothing happened, in which the breath stood still like a bell that has been struck and has not yet sounded.

Then he breathed out.

When he opened his eyes, the Master's face was very still.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quietly, almost toneless] Nine and one.

He said nothing.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|slowly] Bell Breathing. Nine breaths, one held. That is how the founder breathed. That is what his notes say. The notes of which only two pages remain, because the rest burned in a fire sixty years ago. My husband spent twenty years trying to work out from those two pages how the tenth beat goes. He died without knowing. I don't know either. Nobody on this mountain knows.

She leaned forward a little.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|very calm] Who taught you that?

Mok Yeon looked at his hands. He had imagined this question. He had prepared an answer last night, on the training ground, when Seol-a asked him the same thing.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I dreamed it.

The Master looked at him.

He heard her heartbeat. He didn't need Bell Ear for that; the hall was so still he could hear it anyway. And he heard it not change. Not the smallest jump. A heart that had just heard a lie, knew perfectly well it was one, and was not the least bit upset about it.

The Fourth Tone. Undertone. He had forgotten how good she had been at it. He had forgotten so much.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry, after a long pause] If you lie, lie better.

He bowed his head.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, almost gentle] I won't ask again. Everyone on this mountain has something they don't talk about. Dal doesn't talk about his hands. Do-yun doesn't talk about the girl with the tofu. Seol-a has been carrying a letter in her sleeve for three weeks and thinks I don't know. And I don't talk about why eight hooks in the tower are empty.

He looked up.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|curt] But stop forcing yourself to listen at night. I saw the blood on the ladder. If you force Bell Ear with a body that can't carry it, you'll lose your hearing before you're twenty. Starting tomorrow you practice at sunrise, with me. With me, not alone. Understood?

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Understood.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|nodding] Good. And one more thing.

She reached for the pipe and turned it between her fingers without lighting it.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|casually] On the seventh day of next month I'm going down to Hacheon. Cheon Mu-gyeong is speaking at the River Pavilion. The Cheon family wants to ask the small sects for help with the refugees from the north. If we show our faces, we might get work. Protection contracts, well paid. Do-yun wouldn't have to work for people like Pyo anymore.

Mok Yeon felt the cold run from the back of his neck down his spine.

[Mok Yeon|as calmly as he could] I want to come.

She raised an eyebrow.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|mocking] You? To a meeting with the masters of half the province? So you can run away from a dog there?

[Mok Yeon|earnest] I want to come.

She looked at him. He didn't know what she heard. Maybe just his heartbeat, which was fast now, far too fast for someone making a harmless request.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|after a while] Fine. On one condition.

She pointed upward with the pipe stem, at the ceiling, through the ceiling, toward the tower.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] By the seventh day of the seventh month, you ring the morning bell so that when I wake up I don't think someone threw a cow down the stairs. Once. Just once, properly. Then I'll take you.

He thought of the hoarse, clanging noise he had tortured the bell with on the first morning. He thought of how, as a boy, he had never once rung it correctly. Not a single time.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Agreed.

He stood and bowed. He was almost at the door when her voice stopped him.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quieter, in a tone he didn't know] Mok Yeon.

He turned. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the ancestral tablets, at one in particular, third from the right, its gold fresher than the others. Ryu Gang, Seventh Master of the Nine Bells Gate. Her husband.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|slowly] Earlier you wondered why I'm going. You didn't ask, but you thought it. I know Cheon Mu-gyeong. He was here once. Ten years ago, when Ryu Gang was sick and we didn't have the money for medicine. He came up the mountain, a young man with a kind face and a wagon full of silver, and he bought one of our bells.

Mok Yeon stood very still.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|without looking at him] The sixth. The deepest. It was called Dusk. It hangs in the ancestral hall of the Cheon family in Seoan now. He paid more than it was worth. Much more. I bought the medicine, and Ryu Gang died anyway, the winter after.

She set the pipe back on the floor. Very carefully, as if it might break.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|curt, entirely the Master again] Go. You have a bell to ring.

He went. Out in the courtyard he stopped and looked up at the tower, at the eight empty hooks, and for the first time since waking he didn't know what to think.

In his first life he had never learned this.

Cheon Mu-gyeong had one of their bells. He'd had it for ten years. He had bought it when no one on Unmu knew who he was, and he had paid too much for it.

Behind him, in the main hall, he heard the soft scrape of a flint. Then the smell of tobacco.
