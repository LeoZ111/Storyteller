# Chapter 17: The Sound Net

They told Do-yun on the fifth day of the eighth month, in the tower, after sunset.

The Master had said it had to be there. Beneath the bells. Mok Yeon didn't understand why until he saw Do-yun climb the ladder and look around and sit down beneath Dew on the boards, heavily, legs crossed, hands on his knees as in morning stance. Up here, no one had ever been anything but honest.

This time the Master told it. She told it better than Mok Yeon. Shorter, clearer, with the high points in the right places. She told him about Cheon Mu-gyeong and the false note, about the Black Lanterns and the map, about a boy who had hidden inside a bell and the forty-one years after.

Do-yun listened. He didn't interrupt. He spent most of the time looking at his hands.

When the Master finished, it was quiet for a long time. Down in the courtyard Dal was singing as he washed up. Chil was laughing at something.

[Han Do-yun|finally, slowly] At the gate.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Yes.

[Han Do-yun|nodding, as if confirming something] I roared.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] For a long time. Like an ox.

Do-yun thought about it. He thought about it for a long time, the way he thought about everything for a long time, and Mok Yeon waited and listened to his heart, heavy and slow, and it didn't change. No jump, no stumble. It simply kept beating.

[Han Do-yun|after a while, calm] Then this time I'll hold it better.

That was all. He asked no questions about time and bells and how such a thing could be possible. He didn't ask whether it was really true. He looked at Mok Yeon for a long time with his broad, serious face, and then he reached out and laid his hand on Mok Yeon's head, heavy and warm, and ruffled his hair, the way you do with a younger brother.

[Han Do-yun|quietly] Forty-one years alone. That must have been long.

Mok Yeon couldn't answer.

[Han Do-yun|after a pause, practical] What do we need?

***

What they needed was a wall.

The west wall had been collapsed for twenty paces ever since a winter eight years ago when the frost cracked the stones. Since then there had been only thornbrush there, which any billy goat could get over. Do-yun started the next morning. He carried stones. He carried them up from the streambed below the west side, one by one, fieldstones the size of pumpkins, on his back, in a willow frame Dal wove for him. Twenty a day. Thirty. He set them without mortar, the way the old wall had been set, each stone on two others, and knocked them firm with a wooden mallet.

Anyone looking from the goat path saw a big young man finally repairing his sect's wall. With the silver from the Cheon family's protection contract. Which was exactly what anyone in his place would do.

Mok Yeon knew someone was looking. He didn't hear it. He knew it.

***

What else they needed was ears.

The idea came to Mok Yeon one evening as he sat beneath the bells listening to the wind. Dawn and Dew hummed when the wind touched them. Each differently. And if you listened closely you could tell from the hum which way the wind was blowing: in a west wind Dew sang first, in an east wind Dawn.

He went to Dal.

The old man was sitting in the kitchen peeling radishes. He listened without looking up while Mok Yeon talked. About small bells. Dozens. As small as the one he had given Seol-a, or a little bigger. Each tuned differently. Hung on thin threads in the brush along the goat path, on the wall, at the side gate, in the trees along the main path. So that anyone approaching the mountain would brush one. And so that up in the tower, hearing the tone, you would know which one it was. And where.

Dal kept peeling. One radish. Two. Then he put down the knife.

[Dal|growling] The furnace behind the tower has been cold for ninety years.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I know.

[Dal|grumbling] Snakes live in the nettles.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Do-yun isn't afraid of snakes.

[Dal|after a long pause] Bronze costs money.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Madam Ok has two chests of pawned cooking pots nobody has claimed in years. She said she'll sell them to us at the metal price. Because she hates losing bets but likes people who save her warehouse.

Dal looked at him. For the first time he really looked at him, with those small red-rimmed eyes that glinted in the firelight.

[Dal|quietly] How do you know I can tune a bell? Not cast. Tune. Nobody knows that. Not even the Master.

Mok Yeon was silent. In his first life he had learned it from the founder's two pages, at the very bottom of the margin, in a note in a different hand: Young Dal has the ear. He hears when the bronze lies.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] I heard it when you gave Seol-a the little bell. It didn't sound like a piece of Storm. It sounded like Storm.

Dal looked at him a moment longer. Then he stood up, knees cracking, took a bucket and a sickle from the wall, and went out. To the back of the tower. To the nettles.

***

It took them nine days to clear the furnace and four to get it burning again.

It was built of clay and brick, half dug into the slope, with a chimney like a crooked finger. The founder had cast nine bells in it, over nine years. For ninety years it had housed snakes and mice and once, as a nest of feathers proved, an owl. Do-yun removed the snakes, with his bare hands and great courtesy, and carried them down to the valley in a sack. Chil removed the owl's nest and kept the feathers.

Then the furnace burned.

Mok Yeon would never forget it. The smell. Charcoal and hot clay and something sharp and metallic as the first cooking pots from Madam Ok's chests slumped together in the crucible. The heat that came out of the opening like a flat hand. And Dal, standing in front of it without gloves, with his scarred hands that had been burned once before and that he now, after thirty years, held near the fire again.

He was humming. He hummed the tune with no beginning and no end, and Mok Yeon understood for the first time that it had never been a cooking song. It was a casting song. The rhythm for working the bellows.

The first little bell was ugly. Crooked, with a bubble in the metal, and when Dal struck it with a wooden rod it made a sound like an annoyed duck. Dal threw it back into the crucible.

The second was better. The fifth was good. From the tenth on, Dal stopped throwing them back.

Then came the tuning. That was the part no one but Dal could do. He took each little bell once it had cooled and struck it and listened, eyes closed, head tilted like a bird. Then he took a file and scraped. On the inside of the rim, very little. And struck it again. And scraped again. Sometimes a single bell took a whole hour. And in the end each one sounded different, and each one sounded right, and Dal laid them out in a row on a board, from the deepest to the highest, like notes on a staff.

In the end there were forty-seven.

[Bang Chil|awed] Forty-seven. Like the steps.

[Dal|growling] Coincidence.

It wasn't a coincidence. Mok Yeon saw how the old man looked at the bells as he said it, and knew.

***

They hung the bells at night, so no one on the goat path would see where they went.

Do-yun climbed the trees along the main path. Mok Yeon crept through the brush on the west side and tied the little bells to branches with threads of black yarn, which he stretched across the path at ankle height, invisible in the dark. Chil helped, with the Sparrow Step, silently, and he had never looked so serious in his life. He didn't know why they were doing it. The Master had said it was against thieves. Chil had believed it and been proud, and had asked whether he could hang the deepest bell at the side gate.

He could.

Finally Mok Yeon stood up in the tower beside the Master, and Do-yun walked down the goat path. On purpose. Through every thread.

The bells spoke.

One after another. Soft, bright, each different. A note from the west wall. Then one lower on the slope. Then two in quick succession at the bend where the path rounds the rocks. The Master had her eyes closed. She moved her lips silently. She was counting. She traced the path in the air with the pipe stem, note by note, step by step, and when Do-yun reached the stream at the bottom where the last bell hung, the pipe stem pointed exactly there.

Then Do-yun walked back up the path. And the bells sang in reverse.

Dawn and Dew hummed along. Very softly. The third tone, out of the air between. And for a moment, just one, Mok Yeon had the feeling he could hear the whole mountain. Not with Bell Ear. Through the bells. As if the forty-seven little voices on the slope and the two great ones in the tower were a single ear, and he stood at its center.

Then it was over. The whine in his left ear had grown louder for a moment and faded again.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quietly, impressed, eyes still closed] Ryu Gang would have liked that.

***

The side gate got a new bolt.

Do-yun forged it himself, with Dal's help, at the furnace behind the tower. An iron bolt as thick as two fingers that sat in an iron shoe and could only be lifted from inside, with a handle you had to turn first and then pull. No twig in the world could move it from outside.

[Han Do-yun|satisfied, fitting it] Nobody's getting this open. Only from inside.

Mok Yeon stood beside him and looked at it. He nodded.

He didn't think more about it. He would think about it later, very often, about this morning and this sentence. Only from inside. But that morning he only thought that the side gate was safe now, and that Chil had hung the deepest little bell beside it, and that it knocked softly against the wood with every gust of wind.

***

The days grew shorter. The nights cooler. In the valley the millet was harvested, and the morning mist smelled of straw.

On the twelfth day of the eighth month, Do-yun went down to Hacheon and didn't come back until evening. He said nothing. He sat down at the table for twenty and ate his bowl of rice, staring into space and smiling. After a while Chil looked at him. Then Dal. Then the Master.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] Well?

[Han Do-yun|red to the ears] I asked her.

[Bang Chil|shrieking] Who? What? The tofu lady? You asked her? What did she say?

[Han Do-yun|grinning, embarrassed] She said idiot.

It was a yes. Everyone knew it. Chil jumped up and ran around the table twice and hugged Do-yun from behind, and Dal came out of the kitchen and silently set an egg in front of him. A single egg. The Master took the pipe out of her mouth and laughed, a real laugh, loud and rough, and it was the first time in weeks.

Mok Yeon sat very still and looked at the First Disciple.

Do-yun had wanted to wait for Mid-Autumn night. He had wanted to ask her by the river with a lantern in his hand, the way the young people of Hacheon did. He hadn't. He hadn't waited.

Do-yun looked over at him, across the table, over the carved names. He said nothing. But Mok Yeon knew why he hadn't waited, and Do-yun knew that he knew.

***

Seol-a's first letter came on the ninth day of the eighth month. The second on the fourteenth. The third on the nineteenth.

They were short and matter-of-fact, in her neat, narrow hand. The hall was large. The teachers were strict and good. The training ground had a roof. Ui-jin was kind. The Patriarch was kind. She had won twice in lessons and lost once, against a teacher, and learned more from that than from the two wins. At the end of each letter was a line for Chil. Don't eat everything yourself. Stop stealing from Dal. The bell at my throat hums every morning when you ring. I think I can hear you.

In the third letter, at the very end, after the line to Chil, there was one more line. It looked as if it had been added later, in slightly different ink.

The Patriarch doesn't sleep in his own rooms at night. He sleeps in the ancestral hall. Beneath Dusk, under an empty beam. I've seen him there. I don't know why I'm writing you this.

The Master read the line three times. Then she handed the letter to Mok Yeon, and he read it three times as well, and they looked at each other and said nothing.

The fourth letter should have come on the twenty-fourth.

It didn't.

On the twenty-fifth Mok Yeon went down to Madam Ok in Hacheon. The courier was back. He had no letter. No one had left anything for him at the pawnshop in Seoan's north market.

On the twenty-ninth, when the next courier also came back without a letter, the Master stood in the courtyard in the morning in traveling clothes, a bundle over her shoulder and at her hip the sword Mok Yeon hadn't seen on her once since he woke.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, firm] I said if she went a week without writing, I'd come and get her.

Mok Yeon stood in front of her on the flagstones. Behind him stood Do-yun. In the kitchen doorway stood Dal.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] There are sixteen days left.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|nodding] I know. Two days there. Two days back. I'll be home in time.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] And if you hear something in Seoan? In his house?

She looked at him. For a long time. Then she smiled, just barely, and it was the saddest smile he had ever seen on her.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] Then he's won, whatever I do. And I'm fetching my disciple anyway.

She turned and walked toward the gate.

And stopped.

Because down the slope, on the main path, in the trees where Do-yun had hung the little bells, the deepest of them was singing. Then the next. Then the next. Hurried, jumbled, in a rhythm Mok Yeon knew. Not a traveler's step. Not a murderer's.

Hooves.

A single horse, at a gallop, coming up the road. A horse that had been ridden too fast for hours.
