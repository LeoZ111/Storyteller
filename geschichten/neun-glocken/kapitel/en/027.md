# Chapter 27: Half Silence

Seo Hwa-ryeong wasn't afraid of silence. She was afraid of the half.

Her right ear wasn't silent. That was the first thing she had learned in the weeks after Mid-Autumn night. Silence would have been merciful. Instead there was a rushing there, like a river behind a wall, day and night, steady, and beneath it sometimes a high, thin tone that came and went as it pleased. The world reached her only from the left now. When Dal came at her from the right with a bowl, she started. When Chil called from the right, she turned the wrong way. She laughed about it, loudly, so the others would laugh along. And then she went into the main hall and sat before the ancestral tablets and didn't laugh anymore.

It wasn't the ear. An ear was only an ear.

It was that she heard only half now. Half her disciples. Half the mountain. When she stood between Mok Yeon and Do-yun during morning stance, she heard only the one on her left. The other she had to look at. For forty years she hadn't had to look.

***

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|under her breath, before the ancestral tablets] You'd have laughed.

The third tablet from the right. Ryu Gang. Seventh Master of the Nine Bells Gate. Its gold was fresher than the others because she had it repainted every year on the anniversary of his death, by a scribe in Hacheon who charged two coppers and did it so badly it had to be repainted every year.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] You always said I hear too much. That I drive the disciples mad because I hear every lie, every excuse, every sigh. Now I only hear half. Are you satisfied?

The tablet didn't answer. It never did. She had talked to it every evening for ten years anyway, and only now, since she knew what a certain boy in the tower had done for forty-one years, did she no longer feel foolish doing it.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quieter] The boy has the Fourth Tone. At sixteen. Without blood. Beside a bell that was being born. And Dal cried. Did you ever see Dal cry? I didn't. Not in thirty years.

She lit her pipe. She did that more often now. Not because she worried more than before. Because the scrape of the flint and the crackle of tobacco were sounds that came from the left, and she liked hearing sounds she made herself.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|very quietly] Ryu Gang. What do I do with him?

***

The next morning she had Mok Yeon teach.

They stood on the training ground in the mist, as every morning. Do-yun with his arm still in a sling, but standing. Chil, who had come every morning since Bell Bend without having to be called, and who was now earnestly trying to hold the invisible bell in front of his round belly. Seol-a at the edge with her sword.

The Master didn't join them. She sat on the wall where Chil usually sat and lit her pipe.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|too loud] Mok Yeon. Teach them Bell Breathing. Properly. All of them.

He looked at her. She saw his face and nearly laughed, it was so helpless.

[Mok Yeon|slowly] I…

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] You can. You taught it to Seol-a in a single night. You taught it to Chil by a river. Now you'll teach it to them in daylight, with me watching.

He stood in front of the three of them. He stood there, slight, with his bandaged hand, looking like a boy put in front of an exam. He opened his mouth. He closed it again.

[Mok Yeon|finally, to Do-yun] Breathe in nine times.

Do-yun breathed in nine times. Thoroughly. Deliberately. The way he did everything.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Now hold the pause.

Do-yun held his breath. His face turned red. Then redder. Then he breathed out with a snort and looked at Mok Yeon.

[Han Do-yun|panting] What pause?

[Mok Yeon|confused] The tenth. The… the one where nothing happens.

[Han Do-yun|honest] With me, what happens there is I can't breathe.

Chil giggled. Seol-a looked away so as not to laugh.

The Master smoked and watched.

Mok Yeon tried another way. He tried explanations. He explained the heartbeat and the breath and the qi and how they swing together, and he explained it precisely and correctly, in the founder's words, and the longer he explained the more glazed Do-yun's eyes became. Chil started drawing in the sand with a stick. Mok Yeon talked faster. He grew impatient. He said: It's very simple. He said it three times.

The Master took the pipe out of her mouth.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|too loud] Stop.

He stopped.

She climbed down from the wall. She went to him. She stood beside him, to his left so she could hear him, and looked at him.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] How did you learn it?

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] Alone. From two pages. In a cave.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|nodding] How long did it take?

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Nine years. Until the pause.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] And that's why you think it's simple. Because you can do it now. And that's why you think everyone has to learn it the way you did. Alone. With explanations. With words on two pages of paper.

He looked at her. He said nothing.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quieter] Mok Yeon. For forty-one years you had no one to teach. You know everything about bells. You know nothing about disciples.

She turned to Do-yun. She stood behind him. She laid her palm flat on his back between the shoulder blades, exactly the way you lay a hand on a bell to feel whether it's ringing.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, to Do-yun] Breathe.

Do-yun breathed. Once. Twice. And she breathed with him. Not loudly. He didn't hear it. But her hand on his back rose and fell in the same rhythm, and his back felt it, and followed without knowing. Seven. Eight. Nine.

And on the tenth, her hand went still.

Not the breath. The hand. It stopped moving. It simply lay there, warm and heavy, like a bell that has been struck and hasn't yet sounded.

Do-yun's back went still too.

For one heartbeat. Two. And beneath her palm Seo Hwa-ryeong felt the flow of qi in Do-yun's body settle for a tiny moment, like water in a jug when you stop carrying it.

Then he breathed out.

[Han Do-yun|quietly, astonished] Oh.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] That was the pause.

She took her hand away. She looked at Mok Yeon. He stood there staring at her hand.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] People don't learn from words, boy. They learn from hands. Your body had to teach you alone because there was no hand. Now you're the hand.

***

He learned. Slowly.

He laid his hand on Chil's back, and Chil giggled because it tickled, and then stopped giggling and breathed. He laid it on Do-yun's back, who found the pause now every third time, then every second. Seol-a no longer needed a hand. But on the third morning she stood in front of him anyway, without saying anything, and waited, and he laid his hand on her back, and they breathed together.

The Master sat on the wall smoking and watching, and sometimes she didn't watch but only listened, with her left ear, to four people breathing in the same rhythm.

It wasn't half. When she heard it like that, it wasn't half.

***

The wedding was on the second day of the eleventh month.

Min-ji had wanted it in Hacheon, at her father's house, as was proper. The Master had said the Nine Bells Gate hadn't celebrated a wedding in twenty years, and when her First Disciple married, it would be on his mountain. Min-ji's father, the small fat tofu seller, had protested at first. Then he had thought of the rope at Bell Bend and protested no more.

So the courtyard was swept. The moss between the flagstones stayed, because Chil said moss was pretty. Madam Ok sent red silk for the banners and refused to take money for it, and then sent a bill that read: Paid by a lost bet. Dal had been cooking for three days, and the kitchen smelled of ginger and sesame and things Mok Yeon didn't recognize. Chil hung the owl feathers from the furnace on the doors because they brought luck, he said. No one knew where he'd gotten that.

Seol-a didn't help.

That is, she helped. She carried wood and swept and tied banners. But she wasn't there. The Master heard it. With her left ear when Seol-a stood to her left, and with her eyes when she stood to her right. Something beneath Seol-a's heartbeat that had been there since the day at Bell Bend and hadn't gone away.

On the evening before the wedding, the Master found her in the stable.

She was currying Chestnut. The brown gelding with the white blaze stood perfectly still and let her, eyes half closed. He hadn't been ridden since Seol-a came from Seoan on him. She curried him every day.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|to her left, calm] You want to tell him.

The currycomb didn't stop. It only slowed.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly, without turning] Do-yun invited him. To the wedding. Because he came that night. Because he left Chestnut here. Do-yun says anyone who comes on a night like that is family.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] Do-yun has a big heart.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible] If I tell him, he loses his father. If I don't tell him, he loses me. I've thought it through a hundred times, Master. There's no third way.

The Master said nothing for a long time. She watched the currycomb glide over the brown coat.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|finally, quietly] He's already lost his father, Seol-a. The day his father first laid a hand on a man's chest. He just doesn't know it yet.

Seol-a turned around. Her eyes were wet.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm] The question isn't whether he loses him. The question is whether he learns it from someone who loves him. Or someday, on a clear day, on a mountain road, from no one.

Seol-a looked at her. She didn't understand the last sentence. She couldn't. The Master had it from Mok Yeon, from a story about a horse and a fall and a father who sat by a coffin for three days, and she didn't even know exactly why she had said it.

Chestnut snorted and nudged Seol-a's shoulder with his nose.

***

That night a letter came from Seoan.

Madam Ok's courier brought it late, when everyone was already asleep, and the Master read it by the light of the eternal lamp in the main hall, before the third tablet from the right. It was addressed to Do-yun, but Do-yun couldn't read well, and he had asked her to open all his letters ever since he'd learned there were letters one mustn't misunderstand.

The handwriting was neat. A little too neat. Like someone who had been rapped with a ruler as a child for every crooked line.

Dear Han Do-yun. Thank you for the invitation. I'm coming. I'll bring a gift for you and your bride, and collect my horse, if Seol-a will give him to me by then.

My father asked me not to come. He says the roads aren't safe, and Unmu has too many enemies, and a son of the Cheon family shouldn't be seen there too often. He said it very kindly. He says everything very kindly.

I'm coming anyway. Cheon Ui-jin.

The Master read it twice. Then she folded the letter and laid it on the altar beside the third tablet from the right.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|very quietly] Ryu Gang. Tomorrow our First Disciple gets married. And the son is coming.

She heard nothing. Only the rushing on the right, the river behind the wall. And on the left, very faintly, through the door, across the courtyard, from the tower, the hum of three bells in the night wind.
