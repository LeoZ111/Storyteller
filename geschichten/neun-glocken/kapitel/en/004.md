# Chapter 4: Your Fourth Step Is a Lie

Yu Seol-a had re-dyed the tassel on her sword three times, and each time it had come out paler.

Red dye cost money. Real dye, the kind that didn't run in the rain, cost a lot of money. So she boiled madder roots from the garden behind the kitchen until the water was rust brown, dipped the silk threads in it, and hung them to dry in the window, and in the end she had a tassel the color of dried blood. She wore it anyway. A sword without a tassel looked like a sword nobody wanted.

She sat on the edge of her bed in the small room behind the main hall, holding a letter she had folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

The paper was heavy and cream-colored, a kind you couldn't buy in Hacheon. The handwriting was neat, a little too neat, like someone who had been rapped with a ruler as a child for every crooked line. She knew every word by heart.

Esteemed Yu Seol-a. Since I watched you fight the three disciples of the Iron Gate School on the tournament ground in Hacheon this spring, I have not been able to forget your fourth strike. I have told my father about you. The Cheon family offers you a place as a guest disciple in our hall in Seoan, beginning in the eighth month, with board, lodging, and access to our teachers. With us, your talent will be seen. I very much hope for a reply. Cheon Ui-jin.

With us, your talent will be seen.

She folded the letter again and tucked it into her sleeve, where she had carried it for twenty days.

She hadn't told the Master about it.

***

At dinner Han Do-yun told them about the escort agency, and he told it the way he told everything: slowly, thoroughly, with the high points in the wrong places. He spent a long time describing the agency chief's mustache. He imitated the squeak of the wheel three times, until Chil snorted rice out of his nose laughing. Then he set the purse of silver on the table, and everyone went quiet.

The Master didn't open it. She only looked inside and closed it again, and for a while she said nothing.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quiet, controlled] That's rice until autumn.

[Han Do-yun|eager, nodding toward Mok Yeon] It was him. He heard the wheel through the wall.

Seol-a glanced over at Mok Yeon. He was eating rice with salt and didn't seem to hear the conversation, and under his left nostril a thin brown crust was still stuck.

She didn't believe it. Han Do-yun was kind-hearted, and kind-hearted people saw miracles where others saw coincidence. Mok Yeon had come up the mountain at ten, an orphan from a village washed away by a flood. For six years he had achieved nothing. He had cried during training. He had hidden behind the woodpile whenever the Master held tests. And in spring he had run across the Hacheon market from a dog, a small yellow dog with one ear, and half the town had seen it.

Seol-a had been at the market that day too. She'd seen it. She remembered how hot her face had gone.

People already laughed at the sect. She knew what they said. Nine bells, one still hanging, and a coward rings it.

***

She practiced while everyone slept.

She always had, ever since she was nine years old and the Master had pulled her out of a burned warehouse by the river that had once belonged to her father. At night the training ground was hers. At night nobody laughed. At night there was only the moon, the battered wooden posts, and the Bell Strike Sword.

It had nine steps. Nine strikes, one for each bell that had once hung in the tower. Most sects taught sword arts with a hundred forms, with spins and leaps, with names like Dragon Wind or Falling Plum Blossom. The Bell Strike Sword had nine steps, and each was a single, heavy cut. It was slow. It was old-fashioned. People who saw it for the first time took it for a beginner's drill.

Seol-a had learned to do it fast. Faster than the Master taught it, faster than anyone else on the mountain. She had cut out the pauses between the steps until the nine strikes flowed into one another like water, and on the tournament ground in Hacheon that had been enough to beat three Iron Gate disciples who knew twice as many forms as she did.

First step. Second. Third.

The wooden swords on the ground were for beginners; she used her own. The blade sang, a thin bright hiss.

Fourth.

[Mok Yeon|calm, from the dark] Your fourth step is a lie.

She whirled, blade up.

He was sitting on the wall at the edge of the ground, in the shadow of the old pine, so still she had taken him for part of the wall. Legs crossed, hands on his knees, eyes closed. Moonlight fell through the pine needles onto his face and made it older than it was.

[Yu Seol-a|sharp] How long have you been sitting there?

[Mok Yeon|without opening his eyes] Since your second run.

[Yu Seol-a|cold] Go to bed. You have a fever.

[Mok Yeon|calm] On your fourth step you shift your weight before the qi arrives. The blade is faster than your breath. You cover the gap with speed. But the blade sounds hollow. Anyone with Bell Ear can hear there's nothing in it. And strikes right there.

For a moment it was so quiet she could hear the crickets in the grass.

Then she went hot. Not in the face, like at the market. Deeper. In the chest, where the anger lived that she normally kept as carefully folded as the letter in her sleeve.

She walked to the rack at the edge of the ground, took out two wooden swords, and tossed one in a high arc toward the wall.

He didn't catch it. It landed in the grass beside him, and he opened his eyes and looked at it as if someone had thrown him a fish.

[Yu Seol-a|icy] Show me.

***

He was bad.

That was the first thing she noticed, and the relief went so deep she was ashamed of it. He held the wooden sword like someone who hadn't held one in years, point too low, elbow too far from his body. He stood with his feet too close together. When she attacked the first time, with a simple straight thrust from the first step, he didn't dodge elegantly. He only stepped half a pace aside, clumsily, almost stumbling, and her point went past his shoulder.

Luck, she thought.

Second step. A cut from above, heavy, with all her weight behind it. He didn't even raise the wooden sword. He was simply somewhere else already when the cut arrived, a hand's width to the left, and the wood whistled past his ear and struck the ground.

Third step. Fourth.

He wasn't there anymore.

And as her weight went forward, as the blade outran her breath, in exactly the moment he had described, she felt something on her right wrist. Not hard. A tap. The tip of his wooden sword resting on the inside of her wrist, right on the tendon, light as a finger.

If it had been steel, she would have dropped her sword.

She leapt back. Her heart was pounding.

[Yu Seol-a|hissing] Again.

She attacked again, and this time she abandoned the form and fought the way she had fought on the tournament ground, fast and hard and without pause. She was better than him. That was the truth. She was far better. She was faster, stronger, her qi ran clean and strong through meridians she had been stretching for eleven years, while his crawled like a trickle through a body that shook as if it hadn't eaten in days.

She hit him. On the shoulder, on the thigh, once glancing off his cheek. He didn't dodge everything. He couldn't dodge everything.

But he dodged everything that mattered.

And every time she went into the fourth step, no matter how she disguised it, no matter how she mixed it in with other cuts, the tip of his wooden sword rested on her wrist. Once. Twice. A third time. Light as a finger. Like someone tapping a bell to hear whether it's cracked.

On the fourth time she stopped.

She was breathing hard. Her hair had come loose; the white cord hung over her ear. He stood facing her, three paces away, the wooden sword loose in his hand, his face very pale in the moonlight. A thin dark thread ran from his nose over his lip to his chin.

[Yu Seol-a|panting, hoarse] Who taught you that?

He opened his mouth. Then his knees buckled.

He didn't fall dramatically. He simply sat down in the middle of the training ground, like a puppet whose strings someone had cut, and stayed there, the wooden sword across his knees, breathing shallowly.

[Mok Yeon|weak, almost amused] My body is bad.

[Yu Seol-a|stunned] Your body is bad?

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Very bad. You don't have to carry me. I'll sit here until the moon is over there.

He pointed vaguely at the pine.

Seol-a stood there not knowing what to do. It was a feeling she didn't know and didn't like. Then she heard footsteps on the gravel, heavy and even, and Han Do-yun came around the corner of the main hall in his undershirt and trousers, hair rumpled, lantern in hand. He saw the two of them. He saw the blood. He said nothing, simply lifted Mok Yeon the way you lift a sack of rice, slung him over his shoulder, and carried him off.

[Mok Yeon|upside down, muffled] I said I'd stay sitting.

[Han Do-yun|grumbling] And I said you have a fever.

Their voices faded. A door closed. Then it was quiet.

***

Seol-a stayed alone on the training ground.

The moon had moved. It stood above the pine now, exactly where Mok Yeon had pointed. She picked her own sword up off the ground, wiped the dirt from the blade, and took the starting stance.

First step. Second. Third.

She did them slowly. More slowly than she had done them in years. As slowly as the Master had taught her when she was nine, and as she had considered a waste of time ever since.

Fourth.

She closed her eyes and listened.

And there it was. So faint she would never have noticed it if she hadn't known what to look for. The blade cut the air, and the hiss was there, bright and clean as always. But underneath, where in the other eight steps a deep, warm hum resonated, the hum of her own qi running through the steel, on this step there was nothing.

A hollow note. A bell strike without a bell.

She opened her eyes. Her hands were shaking, and they hadn't done that in years.

Eleven years. For eleven years she had practiced this step. She had beaten three opponents with it on the tournament ground. Cheon Ui-jin had written her a letter because he couldn't forget this step.

He had admired a hollow strike.

She pulled the letter from her sleeve and held it up in the moonlight, and the neat characters suddenly seemed very far away. With us, your talent will be seen.

At the edge of the main hall, in the deep shadow beneath the eaves, a small red point glowed for a moment and went out again. The ember of a pipe. Someone had been standing there the whole time, watching.

Seol-a tucked the letter back into her sleeve.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible, to herself] Who taught you that, coward?

No one answered. Only up in the tower the bell called Dawn hummed very softly, as if someone had rested a hand on it in passing.
