# Chapter 13: The Hacheon Gathering

The River Pavilion stood on stilts above the water, and that day it looked like a ship about to cast off.

The Cheon family had decorated it. From the sweeping eaves hung white silk banners with the blue sword over the wave, moving slowly in the wind off the river like sails. Between the pillars hung lanterns of yellow paper, ordinary lanterns meant to be lit in the evening. The floor was laid with straw mats, and on the mats, arranged by rank, sat the masters of the region.

There were more than Mok Yeon had expected. The Iron Gate School with twelve disciples in black leather. The Namha family from the western hills, all with the same hooked nose. A monk from a monastery in the south, so old and so thin he looked like a grasshopper in a robe. Escort agencies. Merchant guilds with their own guards. Two dozen small sects whose names Mok Yeon didn't know and had never remembered in his first life either, because they had all disappeared eventually.

The Nine Bells Gate sat at the very back. On the last mat, beside the railing, where the wind was strongest.

The Master wore her best robe. It was the same dark grey robe as always, but she had washed it and re-sewn the patches at the elbows with new thread. Her hair was pinned in a knot so tight it looked painful. She sat very straight. The pipe was tucked into her belt.

Mok Yeon sat beside her. He was wearing Do-yun's second shirt, which came down past his hands, and Dal had combed his hair with water, against his will.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quietly, without looking at him] You say nothing. You look at no one. If anyone speaks to you, you bow and keep quiet. Understood?

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Understood.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|even quieter] And if you see a dog, you stay seated.

Two mats ahead, a man from the Iron Gate School turned around. A broad man with a black beard who looked as if he had combed the hair from his head down onto his face. Master Pyeon. His disciple had been one of the three Seol-a beat in spring.

[Master Pyeon|loud, jovial] Seo Hwa-ryeong! You're still alive! And you brought the dog boy. Where's your swordswoman? Ah, right. I hear she's going to Seoan. Smart girl.

A few people laughed. The Master bowed politely and said nothing. But Mok Yeon saw her fingers close around her belt, where the pipe was.

***

The drums began.

The same deep, steady beats as the procession in the market. Conversation died. At the entrance of the pavilion, where a bridge led across from the bank, the guards parted.

Cheon Mu-gyeong came across the bridge alone.

He wasn't wearing white. Mok Yeon had expected white, white silk, like on the steps in Mujang. Instead he wore a simple dark blue robe without embroidery, belted with a cloth sash like a teacher's. At his hip hung a sword in a worn scabbard. He didn't walk like a patriarch. He walked like someone entering a house where someone is sick.

Behind him, three steps back, walked Cheon Ui-jin, and his gaze traveled across the mats, across the faces, all the way to the back, to the last mat by the railing. He saw the Master. He saw Mok Yeon. He kept looking. Then his face went blank for a moment, like a room where someone has closed the window again.

Seol-a hadn't come.

Mok Yeon looked at Cheon Mu-gyeong. He had last seen him in the rain, from below, with blood in his mouth. Seventy-nine years old, in white silk, hands in his sleeves. Now he was thirty-eight. His shoulders were broad, his stride firm, and at his temples were grey streaks that made him look older. He had a good face. That was the first thing you noticed, and it wasn't a lie. It was a good face.

Mok Yeon felt his hands begin to shake. He laid them flat on his knees and pressed until it stopped.

***

Cheon Mu-gyeong spoke without notes.

He stood before the mats, not raised up, not on a platform, and spoke in a voice that wasn't loud and still reached the railing. He spoke of the refugees from the north. Of the drought years along the Yellow River that had driven farmers off their land. Of the camps outside the gates of Hacheon and Seoan and Mujang, where people slept under tarps. He spoke of numbers without making them sound like numbers. Three thousand in Hacheon. Eleven thousand in the province. Eight hundred children under ten.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, without pathos] Two weeks ago I was in the camp at the west gate. A boy, maybe six years old, gave me a blade of grass. He had been chewing it all day. He thought I was hungry too.

No one in the pavilion said anything. The monk who looked like a grasshopper had closed his eyes.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|continuing] The Cheon family can't do this alone. I'm not here to collect money. I'm here to ask for your blades. The roads aren't safe. The bandits know refugees can't defend themselves. Every sect, every family that guards a stretch of road between the camps and the towns will receive from the Cheon family rice, silver, and our thanks. And, worth more than that, our word that we will stand by you when you yourselves are in need.

He paused.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|quieter] And the Yun family, whose granary burned four days ago, will not bear its loss alone. The Cheon family will replace every sack. Eight hundred. And those eight hundred sacks will be handed out to the camps tomorrow morning, in the name of the Yun family, who have shown themselves generous in these hard times.

At the very front, on the first mat, an old man in silk was weeping into his hands. Master Yun. Around him people began to applaud. First one, then several, then the whole pavilion, and the applause rolled out over the water, and the white banners moved in the wind.

Mok Yeon didn't applaud.

He listened.

Not with Bell Ear. He had promised, and it would have been pointless anyway. He couldn't reach the Fourth Tone with this body. The false note beneath a true voice could only be heard by someone who had worked hearing into their bones over years. In his first life he hadn't managed it until fifty. And even then, on the steps in Mujang, he hadn't been able to hear Cheon Mu-gyeong's note. He had only known it.

So he didn't look at Cheon Mu-gyeong. He looked at the Master.

She was applauding. Slowly, politely, like everyone. Her face was calm. Her breathing was calm.

And then, in the middle of the applause, as Cheon Mu-gyeong raised a hand to thank them for the honor and smiled as he did it, that good, open, tired smile, Mok Yeon heard her breath catch.

Just once. A single breath that didn't come when it should have.

Her hands kept clapping. Her face stayed calm. But her eyes had changed. They were fixed on Cheon Mu-gyeong, and in them was something Mok Yeon recognized, because he had once seen it reflected in a well, at fifty, on the day he finally understood whom he believed and whom he didn't.

It was horror. Utterly still. Very deep down.

In his first life she had come home pale that evening and not spoken for three days. And then she had gone to him. Alone. To tell him to his face what she had heard.

***

After the speech, Cheon Mu-gyeong walked among the rows.

He did it the way he did everything: unhurried and without regard for rank. He greeted the Iron Gate School and the monk and the guilds, but he also greeted the small sects at the very back, and he knew every name. He asked after sick mothers and new disciples and flooded fields. Behind him walked Ui-jin, smiling and bowing, and sometimes looking back toward the railing.

He came closer. One mat. Two.

The Master had stopped applauding. She sat very straight. Mok Yeon could hear her heart now without wanting to, she was that close. It beat heavy and slow. It beat like the heart of someone making a decision.

He knew her. He knew what she would do. She would stand when Cheon Mu-gyeong was in front of her. She would bow and thank him. And then she would say, quietly, so only he could hear: I would like to speak with you privately, Patriarch. And he would agree, kindly, because he was always kind. And everything would happen the way it had happened.

Cheon Mu-gyeong stood before them.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|warm] Sect Leader Seo. It's been a long time. I heard your bells last night. Two. I thought I must have misheard.

The Master stood. She bowed.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, controlled] Patriarch. It's an honor.

She straightened. She opened her mouth.

Mok Yeon stood too. He did it too fast, too clumsily, his hands lost in Do-yun's overlong sleeves, and in doing so he bumped the small lacquer tray beside the mat that held the teapot and two cups the Cheon family's servants had set out for every sect.

He didn't bump it. He kicked it. Exactly the way you swing a clapper.

The tray flew. The pot flew. The tea, still hot, shot through the air in a brown arc and landed on Cheon Mu-gyeong's dark blue robe, right across the chest, just beneath the breastbone.

For an instant the whole pavilion went silent.

Then Mok Yeon fell to his knees.

[Mok Yeon|loud, stammering, panicked] Forgive me! Forgive me, Patriarch! I, I didn't mean to, the sleeves, I'm so clumsy, I'm always so clumsy, forgive me, forgive me!

He struck his forehead on the mat. Once. Twice. He grabbed the hem of Cheon Mu-gyeong's robe to wipe at it and made it worse. He let his voice crack like a sixteen-year-old about to cry. It wasn't hard. He remembered very clearly what it had been like to be that boy.

Somewhere in front, someone laughed. Loudly. Master Pyeon.

[Master Pyeon|roaring] The dog boy! The dog boy just baptized the Righteous Blade! Seo Hwa-ryeong, where on earth did you find him?

Others joined in. Cautiously at first, because no one knew how the Patriarch would take it. Then louder.

Mok Yeon stayed on his knees, forehead to the mat, and felt the laughter on his back like rain. He knew this laughter. He had heard it in the market in spring, with a yellow dog behind him. He had heard it his whole first life, even when no one was laughing.

It was easier to bear when you knew what it was for.

Then he felt a hand on his shoulder.

It was warm. It was calm. It helped him up, carefully, the way you help a child who has fallen.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|gentle, almost amused] Get up. It's only tea. I've had worse on this robe.

Mok Yeon stood. He kept his head down. He didn't want to look into that face. But the hand was still on his shoulder, and he was as close to Cheon Mu-gyeong now as he had been only once in his first life, on the steps, with his fist beneath that breastbone.

And he heard something.

He didn't want to. He wasn't trying to listen. But some things you hear when you're close enough, whether you want to or not. He heard Cheon Mu-gyeong's heartbeat. Calm, strong, steady. A good heart.

And beneath the heartbeat, very faint, half a swing out of step, as if something else were beating behind it in a different rhythm: a second beat.

Like an echo that doesn't come from the walls.

Mok Yeon froze.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|kind, quiet, only for him] You're the boy who rings the bell. This morning. I heard it all the way at the Yun house. It was very beautiful.

He looked at him. Mok Yeon felt the gaze even with his head lowered, the way you feel sunlight on closed eyelids. It wasn't a hard gaze. It was an attentive one. A gaze that listened.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|quieter still] Mok Yeon, isn't it?

He knew his name.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|sharp, loud] Patriarch, forgive me. I haven't raised the boy well. He isn't well. I'll take him away at once.

She grabbed Mok Yeon by the ear. The left one, where the whine lived, and it hurt a great deal, and he was very grateful to her. She bowed to Cheon Mu-gyeong, deeply, as deeply as the disgrace required, and dragged Mok Yeon after her, through the rows, past the laughing faces, past Master Pyeon wiping tears out of his beard, across the bridge to the bank.

Behind them he heard Cheon Mu-gyeong's voice, calm and warm, ending the laughter with a single sentence.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, to everyone] Leave the boy be. We've all knocked something over. Which of you has never spilled tea, Master Pyeon?

And the whole pavilion laughed again, this time at Master Pyeon, and Mok Yeon knew that in that moment he would almost have loved Cheon Mu-gyeong, if he hadn't known better.

***

The Master didn't let go of his ear until they reached the bank, under the willows, where no one could see them.

She turned to face him. He waited for her to shout at him. He deserved it. He had made the sect a laughingstock in front of the whole region, in front of the Iron Gate School, in front of the monk, in front of the Patriarch, on the very day she had come to win contracts that would have freed Do-yun from people like Pyo.

She didn't shout.

She looked at him for a long time, and her face was very pale. Pale as in his first life, on this evening. Behind her the river glittered. From the pavilion came drums, laughter, voices.

Then she turned back toward the pavilion. She took a step.

Mok Yeon caught her sleeve.

[Mok Yeon|quietly, very clearly] Don't go to him.

She stopped. She didn't turn around. Her sleeve was in his fist, and he felt her arm trembling, very finely, like a bell long after it's been struck.

Under the willows it was very quiet.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|without turning, barely audible] You heard it too.
