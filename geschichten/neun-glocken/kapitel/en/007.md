# Chapter 7: The Pawnshop

Madam Ok's pawnshop was full of things someone had once loved.

They hung from the ceiling on hooks and cords, so thick you had to duck. Parasols of oiled paper, red and yellow and green. Birdcages without birds. A lute with three strings. Swords in scabbards, swords without scabbards, a saber with an ivory grip that looked as if it had once belonged to a general. Wedding robes wrapped in tissue. A child's cradle. A stuffed pheasant with one glass eye. When a draft came through the door, everything turned a little, and the things bumped gently against each other, wood on wood, metal on metal, making a soft, untidy jingle, as if they were whispering among themselves.

Chil stood in the doorway and refused to go any further.

[Bang Chil|whispering, horrified] Ghosts live here. Lots of them. I can hear them.

[Mok Yeon|calm] That's the wind.

[Bang Chil|even quieter] That's what ghosts always say.

He had come along because Dal needed salt, and because he had declared that a bell ghost couldn't be allowed into the market alone, or it might eat children. He had insisted on going first. He had used the Sparrow Step the whole way down the mountain and tripped over roots three times, because he was trying to be silent and watch for ghosts at the same time.

Mok Yeon took his hand and pulled him inside.

Behind a counter of dark wood, on a tall chair, sat a woman cracking sunflower seeds.

She did it with great artistry. A seed between the front teeth, a short crack, and the shell flew in an elegant arc into a brass dish at the end of the counter, where it landed with a bright ping. Crack. Ping. Crack. Ping. A rhythm as steady as a water wheel. She was around fifty, slender and upright, in a robe of dark green silk that looked more expensive than anything the sect owned, and on each of her ten fingers was a jade ring. The rings clicked against each other whenever she reached for the next seed.

Beside her lay an abacus. Its beads were black wood, shiny from handling.

[Madam Ok|without looking up, drawling] Two disciples from Unmu. The ox isn't with you. Neither is the pretty one. So you haven't come to pay.

Crack. Ping.

[Mok Yeon|calm] No.

She looked up. Her eyes were small and black and very alert, like the eyes of a bird on a fence deciding whether the thing moving in the grass is edible.

[Madam Ok|amused] You're the one with the dog.

[Bang Chil|indignant, forgetting the ghosts] He's not the one with the dog anymore! He's…

He stopped, because it occurred to him he didn't know what Mok Yeon was now, and that bell ghost might not be the right thing to tell a pawnbroker.

[Madam Ok|with mock patience] He's what?

[Bang Chil|after a brief struggle] He invented a technique.

[Madam Ok|dry] How thrilling.

***

The bells stood in the back courtyard.

Madam Ok led them out herself, through a narrow passage lined with chests, and Mok Yeon heard her rings click with every step. The courtyard was small and walled. A pomegranate tree grew in one corner, its fruit still green and hard. And beneath it, on two wooden planks, under a tarp of oiled sailcloth gone mossy at the edges: two shapes. Round. Heavy.

Madam Ok pulled away the tarp.

Dew was the smaller one. Slender, almost delicate, with a pattern of vines along the rim twining around the inscription. Hearth was broad and squat like a pot, with a shoulder so thick you could sit on it. Both were green. Not the dull, noble green of the bell called Dawn, which was rung and touched every day, but a sick, blotchy green, with white crusts where rainwater had pooled. Five years under a tarp in a courtyard where no one struck them.

Mok Yeon walked slowly around them. He laid his hand on Hearth's shoulder. The metal was cold and rough.

He knocked. Once, with a knuckle.

Hearth answered with a dull, sleepy hum, as if he'd woken it from a very long dream. And Dew, standing beside it, hummed along. Very softly. Just a breath. The way a person murmurs in their sleep when someone says their name.

Mok Yeon stood completely still.

[Madam Ok|behind him, calm] The Master cried back then. She didn't show it. She turned around, right where you're standing, and looked up into the pomegranate tree, and I pretended to count the coins. But I heard it.

He turned. Madam Ok wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the bells, and for a moment her face was entirely different, softer, older.

[Madam Ok|businesslike again] Eighty nyang. Plus five years' interest, which I'm waiving because I'm a foolish old woman with a soft heart. Eighty. By the tenth day of the seventh month. After that a man from Seoan comes with a wagon. He's paying a hundred and twenty. For the metal.

[Mok Yeon|calm] I have no money.

[Madam Ok|nodding] I thought not.

[Mok Yeon|earnest] But I know something.

Crack. She had produced a seed from her sleeve. Ping. It landed somewhere in the courtyard.

[Madam Ok|eyebrow raised] Go on.

Mok Yeon looked toward the courtyard gate that opened onto River Street. Through the cracks you could see the water glittering.

[Mok Yeon|slowly] You have a warehouse on the river. Down by the wooden piers where the grain barges dock. For the big pledges. Rice, furniture, bolts of cloth.

[Madam Ok|cautious] Everyone knows that.

[Mok Yeon|calm] On the third day of the seventh month, at night, it burns. Not just your warehouse. The whole row. From the Yun family granary to the ropewalk. It starts at the oil barrels behind the ropewalk. By morning everything is gone.

The courtyard went very quiet. A sparrow landed in the pomegranate tree, considered the three people, and flew off again.

Chil had clapped a hand over his mouth.

Madam Ok looked at Mok Yeon for a long time. Then she laughed. It wasn't a mocking laugh. It was a bright, surprised, almost childlike laugh that didn't match her rings at all.

[Madam Ok|amused] A fortune-teller. The boy with the dog has become a fortune-teller. And what do you want for your prophecy?

[Mok Yeon|calm] If it burns and I'm right, you give me Dew. And you extend the term on Hearth until the end of the year.

[Madam Ok|smiling] And if it doesn't burn?

[Mok Yeon|without hesitation] Then I work for you for a year. Without pay. I'll clean your parasols. I'll count your seeds.

[Bang Chil|squeaking] Mok Yeon!

Madam Ok stopped laughing. She reached into her sleeve, took out a seed, looked at it, and put it back without cracking it.

[Madam Ok|slowly, appraising] A year. For a bell that doesn't belong to you.

[Mok Yeon|calm] It belongs to us.

She studied him. He knew she didn't have Bell Ear. She didn't need it. She had sat behind that counter for thirty years, and everyone who came through her door lied to her, about the worth of their things, about why they were giving them up, about the day they would come back for them. She had learned to smell lies the way other people smell rain.

[Madam Ok|finally, dry] Done.

She held out her hand. Mok Yeon took it. The rings were cold.

[Madam Ok|on the way out, offhand] And in case you're wondering: I'm having my warehouse cleared out anyway. Tomorrow. Very quietly.

[Mok Yeon|surprised] Why? You don't believe me.

[Madam Ok|shrugging] A boy who bets a year of his life on a prophecy is either mad or knows something. Either way it's worth twenty nyang in porters.

Crack. Ping.

***

Before they left, Mok Yeon asked one more question.

[Mok Yeon|offhand, at the counter] The Willow Branch inn. Who's staying on the upper floor?

Madam Ok raised her eyes from the abacus. The beads stopped clattering.

[Madam Ok|cautious] Why do you want to know?

[Mok Yeon|calm] Because you know.

She thought about it. Outside a fishmonger was calling out the day's prices.

[Madam Ok|quietly] A polite man. Dressed in grey. He paid for the room three months in advance. In Seoan silver, freshly struck, with the Seoan mint's mark on the back. I know because the innkeeper pawned two of the pieces with me to pay his gambling debts.

She opened a drawer and laid a coin on the counter. It was very bright and very clean. A small pagoda was stamped on the back.

[Madam Ok|thoughtful] He goes walking at night. The innkeeper says he always comes back with wet boots. From the dew. Like someone who's been walking through tall grass.

Mok Yeon looked at the coin. He thought of the goat path below the west wall, the tall wet grass, the two footsteps in the dark.

[Madam Ok|sliding the coin back into the drawer] I sell things, not stories. But that one was free. Now go. Your face is scaring off my customers.

***

Something was happening in the market.

They heard it before they saw it: drums. Not the sloppy drums of a street performer, but steady, deep beats in step. The crowd pushed toward the main street, and Chil, who had forgotten he'd been trembling at ghosts a moment ago, dragged Mok Yeon along by the sleeve.

A procession was coming up the street. At the front, two riders with banners of white silk embroidered with a blue sword over a wave. Behind them servants in clean robes carrying chests, carpets, paper lanterns for the River Pavilion. Guards with polished sabers, but their hands weren't on the hilts, just loose at their sides, like people who don't expect to need them. The crowd murmured. Children ran alongside.

[Bang Chil|excited] The Cheon family! It's the Cheons! They're preparing the big gathering!

In the middle of the procession rode a young man on a brown horse.

He was twenty-one or twenty-two, slim, in a pale blue robe with no embroidery that looked more expensive precisely because it was so plain. An open, friendly face that liked to smile and wasn't smiling right now, because he was concentrating. He held the reins loosely. He didn't look down at the crowd the way the guards did. He looked at them.

At the corner by the tofu stall, an old woman's cart tipped over. Radishes rolled across the street, under the horses' hooves. The guards shouted. The procession halted.

The young man dismounted.

He did it without hesitation and without any flourish, as if it were the most natural thing in the world. He knelt in the street in his pale blue robe and gathered radishes, one after another, and put them back in the cart, and when the old woman tried to bow, he held her by the elbow and shook his head and said something Mok Yeon didn't hear.

He didn't hear it because he wasn't listening. He had promised the Master not to force his hearing, and he was keeping that promise. But he didn't need Bell Ear to see that this young man wasn't putting on a show. His hands were dirty from the street. He didn't wipe them off.

[Bang Chil|awed] Who's that?

[Woman in the crowd|dreamily] Young Master Cheon Ui-jin. The Righteous Blade's son.

Mok Yeon stopped breathing.

Cheon Ui-jin. He knew that name. He had heard it once, in his first life, many years later, in a teahouse in a town whose name he'd forgotten. Two merchants had been talking about the Cheon family, about the father's glory and the son's misfortune. Young Master Cheon had fallen from a horse at twenty-seven, on a mountain road, on a clear day, riding a horse he'd ridden since childhood. He had broken his neck. His father sat by his coffin for three days and let no one in.

Mok Yeon hadn't thought more about it then. Now he watched a young man with dirty hands picking up radishes, and he thought about it a great deal.

And then he saw Seol-a.

She stood on the other side of the street, in the shade of a dyer's shop, a small bundle under her arm. He hadn't known she'd gone into town today. She stood very straight, as always, and her face was perfectly calm, as always, but she was holding the bundle so tightly her knuckles were white.

Cheon Ui-jin straightened, looked around, and saw her.

His whole face changed. It was as if someone had opened a window in a dark room. He handed his horse's reins to a servant, crossed the street, through the crowd, ignoring the guards' shouts, and stopped in front of her. He bowed. Lower than a son of the Cheon family needed to bow to the disciple of a sect with one bell.

Seol-a returned the bow. Then she drew something from her sleeve.

A letter. Folded twice, with clean edges.

Cheon Ui-jin took it as if it might break. He opened it right there in the middle of the street, with the crowd whispering around them, and read. And then he smiled. A wide, open, completely unguarded smile that hid nothing and wanted to hide nothing.

[Bang Chil|whispering, stunned] Is that her sweetheart? Seol-a has a sweetheart? Seol-a?

Mok Yeon didn't answer.

He didn't know what the letter said. But he saw the young man's smile, and he saw Seol-a lower her eyes, and he remembered the eighth month. How she had left in his first life. With a bundle under her arm, without looking back.

She had survived because she left.

He stood in the Hacheon market, between radishes and drums and white banners with a blue sword on them, and for the first time he didn't know what to wish for.
