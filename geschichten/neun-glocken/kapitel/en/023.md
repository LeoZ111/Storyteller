# Chapter 23: The Lanterns' Code

Hwang Ryeom had three broken ribs and a crack in his breastbone, and he rode to Seoan himself anyway.

His men had offered him a litter. He had declined, politely, the way he declined everything. A man carried in a litter is a man people remember. So for two days he sat on a grey horse, very straight, with a cloth bound tight around his chest, breathing shallowly, and with every step the horse took it felt as if someone were striking a cracked bell with an open palm.

He thought about that palm the whole time.

A woman of fifty-eight who drops her sword, lays her cheek against bronze, and strikes. He had watched the sword fall. He had looked for a hair's breadth. In thirty years that had never once happened to him.

***

The Black Lanterns kept their house in Seoan in an old dye works.

It stood in the tanners' quarter, by the stinking arm of the river, where the water ran blue and red and yellow out of the drains and where no one went who didn't have to. A long, low building with great vats in which nothing had been dyed for years, and lines under the roof where cloth had once hung to dry. Now lanterns hung there. Dozens. Black paper. Unlit.

Each one belonged to a job.

Hwang Ryeom collected them. The paper of every lantern his men had carried on a job came here afterward, onto the line, with a small tag attached. A name. A place. A date. Some had hung there for twenty years, faded, brittle. Sometimes at night he walked beneath them, like a monk pacing his prayer flags.

There was no lantern for Unmu.

They all lay torn in a courtyard among moss and dandelions, and a young man in pale blue had held one of them in his hands and asked what had happened here.

***

[Lantern Man|quietly, at the door] Sir.

Hwang Ryeom turned, slowly, because there was no other way.

Seok stood in the doorway. The one with the broken nose. The Third. He was thirty and had been with the Lanterns for twelve years, and he was good, and he was calm, and he walked like a craftsman who knows his work. Now he stood there looking as if he didn't know what to do with his hands.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] Come in.

Seok came. He stopped beneath the lanterns and looked up at them, like a child in a temple for the first time.

[Lantern Man|after a while, quietly] The boy has seen me twice. In the alley at the tinsmith's. And now in the courtyard. And the child. The round one. I had him twice. Once at the side gate. And…

He broke off.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] And?

[Lantern Man|barely audible] And I let him run. Both times. And the second time he ran to the tower and rang the bell, and then the mountain sang, and Kim and Byeon are dead.

Hwang Ryeom looked at him.

[Lantern Man|quietly, without looking up] Sir. Why no children? I never asked. Twelve years. Now I'm asking.

***

It was quiet in the dye works for a long time.

Outside the colored river flowed. Light fell through the cracks in the roof and laid stripes across the old vats.

[Hwang Ryeom|after a while, quietly] I was nine. In a village in the north, on a river that dries up in summer. Bandits came. They burned the village. They killed everyone. My mother. My sisters. The teacher who taught us our characters. I hid in a trough where they collect water for the oxen. Under the water. With a straw in my mouth.

Seok raised his head.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] One of the bandits found me. He saw the straw. He bent over the trough and looked at me through the water. He was very young. Younger than you. And he didn't pull the straw out. He simply walked on.

He was silent.

[Hwang Ryeom|quietly] I don't know why. I never found out. I tracked him down twenty years later, when I already had the Lanterns, and I asked him. He didn't remember. He didn't even remember the village.

Seok waited.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] We don't kill children because we were all once children someone could have killed. And because someone didn't. That's all. It isn't a good reason. But it's the only one I have.

Seok looked at him. Then he looked up again, at the lanterns.

[Lantern Man|barely audible] The boy in the tower said: I've done that once already. When you offered him the bell. I heard it from below.

[Hwang Ryeom|after a pause] Yes.

[Lantern Man|quietly] What did he mean?

Hwang Ryeom didn't answer. He didn't know. He had thought about it for two days on the grey horse, with every step, and he didn't know.

***

In the evening he went to the Patriarch.

Not through the main gate. Through the alley behind the stable, through the back gate Ui-jin had opened for the girl three weeks ago. He knew every path on this estate. He had known it for thirteen years.

The Patriarch was in the ancestral hall. Of course. He sat on the sleeping mat beside Dusk, which stood on its low wooden trestle, his right hand on the bronze. He looked up when Hwang Ryeom came in, and his face in the light of the eternal lamp was tired. Older than three weeks ago. The grey streaks at his temples had widened.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] You're hurt.

[Hwang Ryeom|bowing, slowly] Three ribs. The breastbone. A woman with a bell.

He told him. Everything. He told it matter-of-factly, like a report, number by number, man by man. The open side gate. The big disciple at the gate who didn't fall. The swordswoman on the wall. The old man with the boiling water. The boy in the tower and the child with the rope. The mountain that sang. The silence in the middle of the sound. Twenty men who no longer knew which way was up. The widow who dropped her sword.

And then the rider on the steps.

The Patriarch listened without interrupting. His hand lay on the bell the whole time. When Hwang Ryeom told him about the rider, it didn't move. But Dusk hummed. Very softly. As if someone had breathed against it from inside.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|very quietly, when Hwang had finished] He came back last night. On a lame horse. He came straight to me. Here.

Hwang Ryeom waited.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] He told me everything. About the bandits with black lanterns. About the girl on the wall with blood on her sword. About the mountain that sang. He cried. He said: Father, someone tried to kill them, all of them, and one of the murderers recognized me and said The son. And then he looked at me and said: Why did they know me, Father?

He took his hand off the bell. He looked at it as if it belonged to someone else.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|quietly] I told him every bandit in the province knows his face. And he nodded. And then he said: That's what the Sect Leader said too. Exactly like that.

***

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|after a long pause] He asked me to hunt the bandits. The ones who attacked Unmu. He asked me to send men out to find them and bring them to justice.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] And you promised him.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|nodding] I promised him.

He laughed. Very softly. And Hwang Ryeom, who didn't have the Fourth Tone and never had, heard something beneath it anyway. Not the screaming the widow had spoken of. Something else. Something tired.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, the Patriarch again] No more lanterns. Not on Unmu. Not in Hacheon.

Hwang Ryeom bowed. He had expected it.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|continuing] Master Pyeon of the Iron Gate School had his disciples on the road to Hacheon when the mountain sang. Three of them fell off their horses. One threw up. Pyeon has hated the widow for twenty years, because Ryu Gang once beat him in front of everyone. He has filed a complaint with the Murim Alliance's branch here in Seoan. An art that disturbs and confuses the qi of other martial artists. That is the description of a demonic art. It's in the Alliance statutes, word for word.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] Pyeon did it because you asked him to?

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|gently] Pyeon did it because he believes it was his own idea. On the morning after Mid-Autumn night I wrote him a letter asking him to do nothing of the sort. The sect had been attacked, one should have pity. He filed it that same evening.

Hwang Ryeom looked at him. Thirteen years. He had known this man for thirteen years and was still sometimes surprised.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|continuing] And I will see to it that the inspector they send is Baek Ha-seon.

[Hwang Ryeom|surprised] Baek? She can't be bought. She can't even be persuaded. Three years ago she reported the nephew of an Alliance elder for bribery.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] Exactly. If a corrupt inspector bans Unmu's sound art, no one believes it. If Baek Ha-seon does, everyone does. And she will. Because it's true. The art disturbs the qi of others. Twenty men felt it. My son felt it. His horse stumbled. I don't have to invent anything. I only have to make sure someone honest looks.

***

Hwang Ryeom stood there a long time and said nothing.

Then he did something he hadn't done in thirteen years. He asked a question that wasn't part of the job.

[Hwang Ryeom|quietly] Patriarch. The boy. Mok Yeon. When I offered him the chance to crawl into the bell with the child and live, he said: I've done that once already.

The Patriarch raised his head.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] He said it like an old man. Like someone remembering something. Not like a sixteen-year-old who ran away from a dog.

The eternal lamp flickered. Dusk hummed softly.

The Patriarch looked into the flame for a long time. His right hand had wandered back to his chest, beneath the breastbone, flat, as if pressing something back.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|very quietly] Ryu Gang looked at me ten years ago. And at the gathering in Hacheon, when I helped this boy up, he stood just as still. As if he had heard something. Beneath my heartbeat.

He looked at Hwang Ryeom.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|quietly] Find out who he is. Not what he eats. Who he is.

***

Late that night Hwang Ryeom stood alone in the dye works beneath the lanterns.

He had received a message from Hacheon. From the man he still had there, a clerk with the town watch who for a few coins would tell anything he saw. The message was short.

The Sect Leader did not hand the two dead over to the town watch. She buried them herself. Behind the furnace on Unmu. The old cook puts rice on the graves every morning.

Hwang Ryeom read it twice.

Kim and Byeon. Kim with the braid, from a village in the north, who had been with them four years and was still afraid before every job. Byeon, who told jokes nobody found funny. He'd had to leave them lying there. That was against the code. You brought your dead home. He hadn't been able to, with three broken ribs and the client's son in the courtyard.

They lay behind a furnace on a mountain, and a woman whose ear he had destroyed, or who had destroyed her own ear to throw him out of a tower, had buried them. And an old man put rice on their graves every morning.

Hwang Ryeom folded the message. He took a sheet of black paper from the table and began to fold a lantern. Slowly, carefully, the way he had for thirty years. He finished it and hung it on the line with the others, and on the little tag he wrote:

Unmu. Mid-Autumn. Kim, Byeon. Buried.

He didn't light it.

He stood beneath it a long time, breathing shallowly, and in his chest something struck a cracked bell, and he no longer knew for certain whether it was his ribs.
