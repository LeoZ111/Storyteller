# Chapter 18: Frost in Seoan

In Seoan, it didn't rain on the training ground.

That was the first thing Yu Seol-a noticed, and she was a little ashamed of how much she liked it. The Cheon family's hall stood in the third courtyard of the estate, a long building with a roof of black tiles held up by twenty-four pillars of red lacquer. The floor was wood, sanded so smooth you could slide on it barefoot. On the walls hung weapons, real, good ones, in racks of rosewood. There was a library with three hundred scrolls on the martial arts. There were teachers who looked at you and knew at once what you were doing wrong.

And there was food. Every day. Meat, fish, fresh vegetables, rice as white as snow. On the first evening she caught herself wrapping a piece of chicken in her handkerchief for Chil, before she remembered that Chil was two days' journey away.

The Patriarch greeted her personally on the second day. He came into the hall while she was practicing with the sword master and watched for a while in silence, hands in his sleeves. Then he stepped forward and bowed to her. To her. A guest disciple from a sect with two bells.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|warm] Your fourth strike. Ui-jin didn't exaggerate. But you've changed it. It's slower now. And heavier.

[Yu Seol-a|surprised] You saw it in spring?

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|smiling] I was at the tournament in Hacheon. At the very back. I like going to tournaments where no one expects me. You see what people can do when they don't know someone important is watching.

She liked him. That was the truth. She liked him from the first day. He was what you imagine a father to be when you don't have one. Calm. Attentive. He remembered her name and the name of her sword and that she didn't like ginger.

She wrote it in her letters. The Patriarch is kind.

***

She practiced Bell Breathing every night.

Nine and one. On the roof of her guesthouse, where you could see over the estate walls to the lights of Seoan and beyond them, to the south, the dark line of the hills. She breathed, and she waited in the pause the way Mok Yeon had shown her, and mostly nothing happened. Sometimes the door opened a crack. Then she heard the guards on the wall, and beneath their voices she heard boredom, homesickness, a craving for rice wine. And then the door closed again.

Every morning, just after sunrise, the little bell at her throat hummed. Very softly. So softly she felt it more than heard it, a fine tremor on the skin above her collarbone.

She knew it was impossible. Seoan was two days' journey from Unmu. No bell in the world was that loud.

She listened for it every morning anyway.

***

On the twentieth day of the eighth month there was a family dinner.

It was her first. Ui-jin had insisted. She sat between him and an aunt who talked without pause about the price of silk, at a round table of black lacquer covered with so many dishes you couldn't see the wood. The Patriarch sat across from her. He ate little. He listened. Sometimes he laid his right hand flat on his chest, beneath the breastbone, and took it away again when he noticed someone looking.

She hadn't slept the night before. She had sat on the roof until dawn, breathing. Nine and one. Nine and one. And at some point, as the bell at her throat began to hum, the door had stopped swinging shut.

She only noticed when she came down. The servant who brought her water said: Sleep well, young lady? And beneath his voice she heard: I hope you didn't see me coming out of the storeroom. Perfectly clearly. Not in words. Like a second melody beneath the first.

All day she had heard lies. Small ones. Harmless. I'm not tired. It's delicious. Of course I read the book. The world was full of small, harmless lies, and she had never known. It was as if someone had peeled off her skin.

Ui-jin was telling a story. Something about his fencing teacher getting tangled in a curtain, and she listened to him, and beneath his voice there was nothing. Nothing at all. His voice was the same as his heart. She nearly wept with relief.

The Patriarch laughed.

It was a lovely laugh. Warm, a little tired. He tipped his head back and laughed at his son, and as he did the qi inside him moved, only a little, like water in a jug that's been bumped.

And beneath it, people were screaming.

Many of them. Far away. As if behind a thick door. They didn't scream in words. They screamed the way animals scream when they're slaughtered, and then quieter, and then they stopped, and others began. It was in his laugh. It was beneath his laugh. It was the laugh.

The teacup fell from her hand.

It shattered on the black lacquer. The tea ran across the table, between the dishes, onto the aunt's robe. Everyone looked at her.

[Cheon Ui-jin|alarmed] Seol-a? What is it?

[Yu Seol-a|flat] Nothing. Forgive me. I'm tired.

Beneath her own voice she heard the lie. For the first time in her life she heard herself lying.

The Patriarch looked at her. Kindly. Concerned. He leaned forward.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|gently] You're pale. Go and rest. Ui-jin, take her to her room.

She stood. She bowed. She walked out. She made it as far as the second courtyard, behind a lilac bush where no one could see her, and there she threw up.

***

In the nights that followed, she didn't sleep.

She lay in her room and listened. She had learned to tune out the small lies, the way you tune out the rushing of a river once you've lived beside it long enough. Beneath them, deeper, she searched for the other sound. The sound from the laugh.

She found it on the third night.

It was after midnight. She heard footsteps in the courtyard. Calm, even, the steps of a man who knows where he's going. She got up, went to the window, and saw him: the Patriarch, in a plain sleeping robe, a lantern in his hand. He came out of the ancestral hall. He crossed the second courtyard into the third, past the training hall, toward the stable.

She followed him. She didn't use the Sparrow Step; she couldn't. But she could be quiet. Eleven years of practicing at night had taught her to be quiet.

Behind the stable there was a staircase. It led down to a cellar she didn't know, behind a door of iron. The lantern disappeared inside. The door closed.

Seol-a crouched in the shadow of the stable wall. She breathed. Nine and one. She waited in the pause.

Through the iron she heard heartbeats.

Three. No, four. One of them calm and strong: the Patriarch's. The others weak. Irregular. Like the hearts of people who have been sick for a very long time. And then she heard one of the weak heartbeats falter. Heard it contract as if something were being drawn out of it, like water from a well. And heard the strong heartbeat grow calmer at the same time. Relieved.

Under the door, in the narrow gap above the threshold, lay a sliver of lantern light. And in it, very briefly, a reddish haze that rose and vanished.

Then a voice. His voice. Calm, warm, kind.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|behind the door, muffled] Thank you.

Seol-a ran.

***

On the twenty-fourth day she wrote the fourth letter.

She put everything in it. The laugh. The cellar. The heartbeats. The thank you. She wrote with a trembling hand, and the writing wasn't neat and narrow anymore; it was scrawled and crooked, like a child's. At the end she wrote no line to Chil. She couldn't.

She folded the letter twice, tucked it into her sleeve, and went to the north market.

The pawnshop in the north market was smaller than Madam Ok's, but it smelled the same. Of old cloth and lamp oil and things someone had once loved. Behind the counter sat a young man with an abacus. And at a small table beside the door, where customers waited for their appraisals, sat another man drinking tea.

He wore grey. He had a friendly, clean-shaven face that recalled a village schoolteacher. She had seen him before. On Unmu, a month ago, as the Cheon family's steward with a blue band on his sleeve. He had been studying the west wall.

He looked up when she came in. He smiled. Politely. A little sadly.

[Hwang Ryeom|friendly, half rising] Young Lady Yu. What a coincidence. A letter for home?

She heard his voice. And beneath his voice she heard nothing. Nothing at all. No lie, no truth. A smooth grey surface, like water in a well that no light falls on. She had never heard anyone who sounded like that. As if he had learned to have nothing beneath his voice.

[Yu Seol-a|calm, and she heard the lie in her own voice] No. I want to pawn something.

She went to the counter. She pulled the first thing she found from her belt. The tassel from her sword. Red as dried blood, from madder roots out of Dal's garden. She laid it on the counter.

The young man with the abacus looked at her as if she had handed him a dead bird.

[Pawnbroker|confused] That's… a tassel.

[Yu Seol-a|cold] Yes.

He gave her two copper coins. She took them and left, and she felt the man in grey's eyes on her back until she turned the corner.

She burned the letter that night in the oil lamp in her room, line by line.

***

After that, she couldn't write anymore.

She didn't know whether she was being watched. She only knew she couldn't rule it out. Any servant who brought her water could be one of them. Any courier. Any man at any table in any pawnshop.

Ui-jin noticed something was wrong. Of course he did. He came to her every day with a book or a piece of fruit or a question about her fourth step, and she looked into his face and heard his heart, which was the same as his voice, and she couldn't tell him.

Mok Yeon had said it on the roof. Ui-jin can't hide anything. He never learned how.

If she told him, he would go to his father. Not to betray her. To ask. To tell his father it had to be a misunderstanding, an illness, a bad dream. Because he loved his father. And his father would answer him, gently and calmly, and beneath the answer people would be screaming, and Ui-jin wouldn't hear it.

[Cheon Ui-jin|worried, at her door] Seol-a. Are you ill? Should I fetch a doctor? My father says you look like someone who's homesick.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Maybe I am homesick.

It wasn't a lie. She heard it herself.

***

On the night of the twenty-seventh, she went into the ancestral hall.

She didn't know why. Maybe homesickness. Maybe because she wanted to hear whether the bell called Dusk still sounded like a bell from Unmu after ten years in this house.

The ancestral hall was large and silent. Hundreds of ancestral tablets, row upon row, gold on black, in the light of a single eternal lamp. And in the middle of the room, on a low wooden trestle because its beam had been sent to Unmu, stood Dusk.

It was enormous. Bigger than Dawn, bigger than Dew. Dark green, almost black, with a shoulder as broad as a table. On the floor beside it lay a sleeping mat. A pillow. A folded blanket.

Seol-a stepped closer. She laid her hand on the bronze.

Dusk hummed. Deep, very deep. So deep it was more a feeling in the belly than a sound. And at her throat the little bell from Storm answered, bright and trembling, like a child in a dark house hearing someone it knows call out.

Then she heard footsteps.

Two pairs. One calm and strong. One she didn't hear, only sensed, like a shadow sliding across a wall.

She vanished behind the front row of ancestral tablets. She crouched in the gap between the altar and the wall, as small as she could make herself, and she thought, suddenly and completely out of place, of Mok Yeon. Of a sixteen-year-old boy inside a bell. She didn't know why she thought of it. She had never heard that story.

The door opened. The Patriarch came in, a lantern in his hand. Behind him, the man in grey.

The Patriarch sat down on the sleeping mat beside Dusk. He laid his right hand on the bronze. He closed his eyes. For a long time neither of them said anything.

[Hwang Ryeom|quiet, matter-of-fact] The widow postponed the bell. The beams aren't rotten. The west wall has been reset, in three weeks, by the big disciple. On the goat path there are little bells on threads. Forty-seven. My man walked through one on the fourth night and heard someone get up in the tower. And the boy has the map.

The Patriarch said nothing.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] She hasn't said anything. To anyone. She hasn't gone to the Alliance or to the other sects. She accepted your contract and your silver and bowed to your son.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|quietly] But she heard.

[Hwang Ryeom|after a pause] I believe so.

The hand on the bronze didn't move. Dusk hummed. Seol-a felt it in her bones.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|very quietly] Ryu Gang heard too. Ten years ago. He looked at me and said nothing. And then he died, and I thought the silence died with him. I bought his bell. For ten years I've slept beside it, because it's the only thing in the world that calms me.

He laughed softly. And beneath the laugh the people screamed, and Seol-a bit her hand until she tasted blood.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, final] The fifteenth. The night of the Mid-Autumn Festival. All of Hacheon will be at the river. No one will look up.

[Hwang Ryeom|matter-of-fact] And the bells?

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|after a pause] No fire at the tower. The bells stay hanging. I'll fetch them afterward.

[Hwang Ryeom|quieter] And the girl? She's in your house.

A long silence.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|gently] She stays in my house. Ui-jin loves her. Nothing will happen to her. She doesn't leave Seoan until it's over. Afterward she'll grieve, and Ui-jin will comfort her, and one day she'll understand that she has a home here.

Seol-a closed her eyes.

***

She fled that same night.

She took nothing with her. Her sword, the little bell at her throat, nothing else. She didn't go through the courtyards; she went over the roofs, the way she did on Unmu, and dropped down at the back of the stable, and went inside to the horses snorting in the dark.

Someone was standing there.

Ui-jin. In his sleeping robe, barefoot, a lantern in his hand. He had been standing in front of his horse's stall, a brown gelding with a white blaze that he had ridden since he was ten. He turned when she came in. He saw the sword. He saw her face.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quietly, confused] I couldn't sleep. I saw you on the roof.

She couldn't say anything. She stood there three paces from him, and the lantern shook in his hand, and he looked at her with that open face with nothing beneath it, nothing at all, only worry.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible] Let me go. Please. Don't ask.

He looked at her for a long time. He had no Bell Ear. He couldn't hear undertones. But he wasn't stupid. He saw that she had been crying. He saw that she was shaking. He saw the blood on her hand where she had bitten it.

[Cheon Ui-jin|very quietly] Is it because of me?

[Yu Seol-a|honest, broken] No. Never because of you.

He heard no undertone. But he believed her. She saw it. She saw him believe her without understanding what he believed.

He turned, opened the stall, and led out the brown gelding. He saddled it himself, quickly and deftly, with the hands of a boy who had done it every morning for ten years. Then he handed her the reins.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quietly] His name is Chestnut. He's the fastest in the stable. He doesn't like the whip. Talk to him, and he'll run.

[Yu Seol-a|whispering] Ui-jin…

[Cheon Ui-jin|with a crooked smile] I'll follow. I'll come and get my horse back. And then you'll explain everything to me. Promise?

She couldn't promise. She mounted. He opened the back gate of the stable yard that led into the alley, and she rode out into the darkness, into the sleeping city, and behind her a young man in a sleeping robe stood in the alley holding a lantern.

***

She rode for a day and a night. She slept for an hour in a barn. She talked to Chestnut, as Ui-jin had told her to, and he ran.

On the morning of the twenty-ninth she saw Unmu. The narrow tower, too tall for the other buildings. She urged Chestnut up the road, past the bend where the steps began, and in the trees beside the path little bells suddenly rang, one after another, bright and hurried, as if they were calling her name.

The Master stood at the gate. In traveling clothes, with a bundle over her shoulder and a sword at her hip. Behind her, Do-yun. Behind him, Dal, in the kitchen doorway.

And Mok Yeon.

Seol-a let herself fall from the horse. Her legs wouldn't hold her. The Master caught her with both arms and held on to her, and Seol-a smelled tobacco and moth herbs and the smell of her childhood.

[Yu Seol-a|gasping, into the Master's shoulder] The fifteenth. Master. They're coming on the fifteenth. On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival.

She raised her head. Over the Master's shoulder she saw Mok Yeon. He stood very still. His face was very pale.

He didn't look surprised.

[Yu Seol-a|flat] You knew.

He didn't answer. But she stood at the Fourth Tone now, and she heard his silence, and beneath it she heard no lie. Only an old, old pain, like a bell struck a very long time ago that has never stopped ringing.
