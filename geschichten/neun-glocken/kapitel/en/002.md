# Chapter 2: Rice with Salt

The kitchen of the Nine Bells Gate was the only room in the sect that never went cold.

That was Dal's doing. Dal was past seventy, came up barely to Mok Yeon's shoulder, and had hands like two burnt roots, brown and glossy with scars up past the wrists. No one knew exactly when he had come to the mountain. He had simply always been there, like the stove he stood at, a hulking brick thing with three fire holes and black iron pots steaming above them. He stirred them with a wooden spoon as long as his arm and hummed tunes that had no beginning and no end.

He set a bowl of ginger water in front of Mok Yeon without looking at him.

[Dal|growling] Drink. The Master says you have a fever. Chil says you have a ghost. I say you ate the sour plums off the tree behind the latrine again last night.

[Mok Yeon|slowly] Thank you, Dal.

Dal stopped stirring. He half turned. Something worked in his wrinkled face, as if he'd heard a wrong note in a familiar tune.

[Dal|suspicious] Thank you? Since when do you say thank you?

Mok Yeon didn't know. He no longer knew what kind of boy he had been at sixteen. He remembered the fear. The feeling that everyone else could do something he couldn't. A dog in spring that he had run from, straight across the Hacheon market, and the laughter afterward. He didn't remember gratitude.

He drank the ginger water. It burned his throat and tasted of Dal.

***

The six of them ate at a table built for twenty.

It stood in the dining hall beside the kitchen, a long dark slab of camphor wood into which generations of disciples had carved their names. The Master sat at the head. To her right, Han Do-yun; to her left, Yu Seol-a; then Chil, then Mok Yeon. Dal never ate with them. He stood in the doorway and chewed standing up, because he said sitting made you lazy.

Fourteen places stayed empty. The names carved there were old, the grooves gone soft.

Mok Yeon looked at Han Do-yun.

The First Disciple was twenty-five, broad as a doorframe, with a square jaw and hands that made a rice bowl look like a teacup. He wore his hair cropped short, which was rare among martial artists, because he worked as an escort guard for merchants and long hair got in the way in a fight. He chewed slowly and thoroughly, the way he did everything. A fresh scrape ran above his right eyebrow, crusted over.

Mok Yeon had heard him roar. Deep and long, like an ox. And then not anymore.

[Han Do-yun|good-natured, mouth full] What? Do I have rice on my face?

[Mok Yeon|quietly] No.

[Han Do-yun|grinning, to Chil] He's looking at me like I owe him money.

[Bang Chil|eagerly] He cried this morning! And hugged me! I think a bell ghost got into him. A really old one. One that never hugged anybody its whole life and now wants to make up for it.

Han Do-yun laughed. It was a loud, clumsy laugh that sounded like someone tumbling down a wooden staircase, and it ended in a snort through the nose.

Mok Yeon felt his eyes sting.

He ducked his head over his bowl, but it was too late. Two drops fell into the rice. Chil saw it and elbowed the First Disciple with all the force of a boy who has discovered something important.

[Bang Chil|whispering, loud enough for everyone] See? See? Again!

[Han Do-yun|thrown] Hey. I wasn't laughing at you. I was laughing at Chil.

[Mok Yeon|hoarse] I know.

[Han Do-yun|after a pause, awkwardly] Do you want my egg?

There was one egg. A single one, for the First Disciple, because he did the hardest work and brought home the most money. Do-yun pushed it across the table with his chopsticks, over the carved names, until it sat beside Mok Yeon's bowl.

[Mok Yeon|after a moment] Is there salt?

Everyone looked at him.

Dal brought the salt dish in silence, with the expression of a man who has decided to stop being surprised by anything. Mok Yeon sprinkled salt over his rice, a thin, even layer, the way he had done for forty-one years in caves, on ledges, beside campfires where no one but him ever sat. Salt kept forever. Rice could be found in any village. He had never needed more.

He ate. He didn't touch the egg. He slid it back when Do-yun wasn't looking.

At the other end of the table, Yu Seol-a set down her chopsticks. They clicked, one clean note.

She was twenty and the only one at that table who looked like she belonged in a bigger sect. Tall, straight-backed, her black hair tied high with a white cord whose ends fell between her shoulder blades. Her face was narrow and pale, and her eyes were so dark and calm you couldn't tell whether she despised you or simply saw you. At her hip hung a sword with a worn grip and a tassel that had once been red.

She was the most gifted of them. Everyone knew it. So did she.

[Yu Seol-a|cool, not looking at Mok Yeon] Master. If he's sick, he should stay in bed. If he isn't, he should stop disrupting the meal. And someone else should ring the morning bell. People in Hacheon are already laughing at us. We don't need to give them another reason.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, pipe beside her bowl] People in Hacheon have been laughing at us since your grandfather was a child. Eat your vegetables.

Seol-a ate her vegetables. But first she looked at Mok Yeon, just once, and it was a look he remembered. Not contempt. Something worse: indifference making an effort to stay polite.

In his first life, she had left the sect in the eighth month. She had gone to Seoan. She had survived because she wasn't here. Later he'd heard rumors of a swordswoman they called the Frost Blossom Blade, and later still, nothing. He had never found out what became of her.

He had never tried. That was the truth. He'd been afraid she would ask him where he had been that night.

The Master said nothing more. She ate slowly and watched him over the rim of her bowl, and he felt her gaze like a hand resting on a bell.

***

He spent the rest of the day walking the mountain.

He did it the way a hermit walks a new cave: slowly, fingers on the walls. He counted the steps from the main gate to the courtyard: forty-seven. He checked the wall that ringed the sect, a band of stacked fieldstones, collapsed on the west side and patched with thornbrush. Behind the kitchen he found the side gate, a narrow wooden door with a bolt so loose a child could have lifted it from outside with a twig.

He stood in front of that door for a long time.

That night, the main gate had broken. He knew that. He'd heard it. But before the gate broke, he had also heard something else, something quieter, which for forty-one years he had never been able to place: wood creaking. At the back. By the kitchen.

Had the first men come through this door?

He didn't know. He knew so little. After that night he had spent forty years in the mountains, with nothing but an art no one taught anymore and a name he learned only very late. He knew the date. He knew the black lanterns. He knew the Master had gone down to Hacheon in the seventh month for a great gathering, and that she had come back pale and not spoken for three days.

And he knew the name of the man who had sent the lanterns. It had taken him twenty years to find it, and another twenty to believe it.

Cheon Mu-gyeong.

Thirty-eight years old now, Patriarch of the Cheon family of Seoan, a hero whose name children sang in the market. And Mok Yeon was a sixteen-year-old boy who had run from a dog.

If he told the Master, she wouldn't believe him. And if she did believe him, she would go to Cheon Mu-gyeong and confront him. That was who she was. And then the lanterns would come, perhaps sooner than the first time.

[Mok Yeon|thoughts] So the body first. Without a body, I'm just a boy with bad dreams.

***

That night he climbed the bell tower.

The moon hung behind thin clouds and turned the mist in the valley into a silver lake. The bell called Dawn hung motionless in the beams, and when he stepped beneath it he smelled copper and old grease, and for one heartbeat he was sixteen again, small, in the dark.

He sat beneath it, legs crossed, back straight, hands on his knees. He closed his eyes.

The First Tone came at once.

His own heartbeat, loud and clear, as if someone had set a drum beside his ear. He had forgotten how fast a sixteen-year-old's heart beats. How much blood was in it, how much impatience. He heard it with an old man's understanding and thought: This is a heart that has never lost anything.

He breathed. He let the breath sink into the heartbeat, slowly, until both swung to the same measure, and felt the thin trickle of qi stir in his lower belly, little more than a warm thread. The Second Tone. For a beginner, months of work. For him, remembering.

Then he tried the third.

Bell Ear is the stage at which a disciple stops hearing only himself. He lets go of his own vibration like a stone dropped into a pond and listens for the rings that come back from others. Mok Yeon knew exactly how it was done. He had done it ten thousand times.

He dropped the stone.

And for one moment he heard the world.

He heard Chil snoring, a wet, rattling whistle broken by lip-smacking. He heard Han Do-yun's heart, slow and heavy as an ox's tread. He heard Dal mumbling in his sleep, always the same three notes. He heard the Master awake in the main hall, her breathing shallow, the faint crackle of tobacco in the pipe she only lit when she was worried. And behind the hall, on the dark training ground, he heard the hiss of a blade cutting air, again and again and again: Yu Seol-a, practicing while everyone slept.

Then something tore inside his head.

A high, whining tone, so shrill he clenched his teeth. Warmth ran from his left ear down his neck. He pressed his hand against it and felt it come away wet.

Hearing is understanding. Acting requires a body. It was written in the founder's teachings, right at the beginning, and he had read it a thousand times and never understood it the way he did now. His understanding was an old man's, but his meridians were a boy's: narrow, clogged, never stretched. He had tried to send a river through a straw.

He sat until the whining faded. It didn't fade completely. A thin, high note remained, like a mosquito circling somewhere in the dark.

He leaned his forehead against the pale patch on the bronze, where generations of disciples had rested theirs.

[Mok Yeon|whispering] Ninety days. Eighty-nine, starting tomorrow. This time I won't use you as a hiding place. Do you hear me?

The bell hummed. Barely. Maybe it was the wind.

Then he heard it.

Not with Bell Ear. That was spent. With the ordinary ears of a boy, which are very good in the silence of a mountain at night. Down the slope, past the collapsed west wall, where the old goat path wound through the brush: a footstep. Light. Far too light for a farmer, far too deliberate for an animal. A second. Then nothing.

Someone was standing down there in the dark, not moving.

Mok Yeon held his breath. The blood on his neck went cold.

For a long time nothing happened. Then, so softly he wasn't sure he hadn't imagined it, the footsteps moved away again, downhill, and only the mist remained.

He was still sitting there when the sky turned grey. In his first life, he had believed the men with the lanterns came out of nowhere that night.

Maybe they had been there all along.
