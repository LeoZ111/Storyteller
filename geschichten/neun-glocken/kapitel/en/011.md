# Chapter 11: The Wall

It had taken Han Do-yun three years to hear his own heartbeat.

Three years. Seol-a had needed four months. Chil, who never practiced and fell asleep during stance training, had managed it after one year, almost by accident, napping on a flour sack. Do-yun had stood every morning before the others woke and every evening after they went to bed, and when he finally heard it, that dull, steady pounding in his chest, he had been so happy that he told no one. He had been afraid they would laugh because it had taken so long.

He was no talent. He knew that. It didn't bother him, mostly. Someone had to guard the wagons, carry the tiles up to the roof, chop the wood, bring the purses of silver home. Someone had to be the ox.

But sometimes, when he woke at night and heard the others breathing, he thought about what Mok Yeon had said to Min-ji. He won't die. And he wondered why a boy would say something like that, as if he knew.

***

On the fifth day after the fire, Madam Ok sent for him.

She had a job. The rescued pledges from the warehouse, chests of silk and porcelain and stored heirlooms, had to be moved to a new warehouse in Wolgok, a market town half a day to the west, where a cousin of hers owned a house with a dry cellar. Two wagons. Half a day there, half a day back. Twelve nyang.

[Madam Ok|drawling, cracking seeds] Twelve. Because I like you. And because the fortune-teller is coming along.

[Han Do-yun|confused] Mok Yeon? He's hurt.

[Madam Ok|calm] He won't be fighting. He'll ride along and tell me whether it's going to rain.

The Master was against it. She stood in the courtyard with her arms folded and said a boy with stitched ribs belonged on a straw mat, not on a wagon seat. Mok Yeon said nothing. He only looked at her. And after a long pause, in which Do-yun didn't understand what was passing between them, the Master took the pipe out of her mouth and said:

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|curt] Do-yun leads. You do what he says. If he says run, you run.

[Mok Yeon|earnest] Yes.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|to Do-yun, quieter] And you bring him back to me.

Do-yun nodded. He had the feeling she meant something other than what she said, and he didn't know what.

***

Min-ji was waiting at the corner by the tofu stall when the wagons came past.

She stood there as if by chance, holding a bundle wrapped in a blue cloth. When Do-yun's wagon drew level with her, she stepped up to the seat and held it out to him without looking at him.

[Min-ji|offhand, too loud] Tofu with spring onions. And rice. There was some left over.

[Han Do-yun|taking it, red] Thank you.

[Min-ji|still not looking at him] Come back, idiot.

She turned and walked away. Do-yun watched her until the wagon rounded the corner. The bundle sat warm on his knees.

Beside him on the seat sat Mok Yeon, one hand on his ribs, looking very attentively in a completely different direction.

***

The road to Wolgok ran through hills.

First through rice paddies where the water stood like mirrors and herons waded with their legs drawn up. Then through a wood of pine and oak where the air grew cool and smelled of resin. Then, after midday, through a gorge. The rock walls closed in, the road narrowed until it was barely wider than a wagon, and on one side a stream rushed below.

Do-yun didn't like this gorge. He had driven it three times. The second time, bandits had come.

He felt Mok Yeon go still beside him. Not calm. Still. Like an animal that stops chewing.

[Mok Yeon|very quietly] Don't stop the horses. Keep going. Slowly.

[Han Do-yun|just as quietly] How many?

He hadn't asked whether. He only noticed that afterward.

[Mok Yeon|after a moment] Nine. Six ahead, past the bend, on both sides. Three up on the slope, left. The ones above have bows. One of them is coughing.

Do-yun let the drop fall. He did it the way Mok Yeon had shown him during stance training the past few days, not forcing it, just listening, feeling outward. And he heard something. Not nine. Maybe four. A restless heart behind a rock. Breathing that was too fast. And from above, on the left, a cough. Wet. Sick.

He wouldn't have heard them. Alone, he would have heard them only when they screamed.

[Han Do-yun|quietly, to the drivers behind him] When I shout, get under the wagons. Not before.

The drivers went pale. One nodded. The other prayed.

Then came the bend.

***

They came from the front, as Mok Yeon had said. Six men in rags with sabers, clubs, and a flail. They leapt from behind rocks onto the road, shouting to spook the horses, and the horses spooked. The wagon lurched. An arrow whistled down from above and struck the wagon seat a hand's width from Do-yun's knee.

[Han Do-yun|roaring] Under the wagons!

He jumped down from the seat. His sword was already in his hand; he didn't know when he'd drawn it. It was a bad sword, heavy and blunt, a blade for people without much money. He planted himself in front of the horses.

The bandits saw him and hesitated. He was big. Bigger than any of them. But there were six of them and one of him, and they were hungry, and you could see it.

[Bandit|hoarse, desperate] Give us the wagons! We don't want to kill you! Hand them over!

[Second Bandit|shrill] Since the boss disappeared we've got nothing! For a month! He just vanished! Give us something!

Do-yun didn't understand what that meant. He only saw their faces. Hollow cheeks. Eyes too big.

Then they attacked.

They were poor fighters. He knew that after the first blow. Farmers with sabers who had once been farmers without sabers. But there were six, and they came from three sides, and arrows came from above.

[Mok Yeon|from the wagon, calm, clear] Left. Two breaths.

Do-yun turned left without thinking. A club came exactly where Mok Yeon had said, exactly when. He caught it on the flat of his blade and shoved the man away with his shoulder.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Front. High.

A saber from above. He raised his sword. Steel on steel, an ugly, shrieking sound.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Right, low. Now.

He stepped aside. The flail struck empty air, the dust where his feet had just been.

It was like dancing. He had never danced, but this was how he imagined it: someone tells you where to put your foot, and you put it there, and suddenly everything fits. He moved through the bandits as through a room whose furniture he knew. He killed no one. He knocked sabers out of hands, clubs to the ground, men into the dust. One ran away. Then another.

An arrow grazed his shoulder. He barely felt it.

And then Mok Yeon said nothing more.

Do-yun waited for the next word. It didn't come. He half turned, and Mok Yeon was sitting on the wagon, one hand on his ribs, looking at him. Perfectly calm. He said nothing because he didn't want to.

The biggest of the bandits came toward Do-yun. He was different from the others. He didn't move like a farmer. He had a real saber, broad and curved, well cared for, and he held it the way you hold a saber when you've learned how. Maybe he'd once been a soldier. Maybe an escort guard like Do-yun himself, before he switched sides.

Behind Do-yun was the wagon. Under the wagon lay two drivers, praying. On the wagon sat a boy with stitched ribs who twenty days ago couldn't even manage the First Tone.

He won't die, the boy had said.

Do-yun thought: No. I'll make sure it's true.

He didn't raise the sword. He just stood. Feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, like stance training in the morning, the invisible bell in front of his chest. He felt his heart. Slow, heavy, steady, like an ox's tread. He breathed. He didn't think about tones or stages or meridians. He thought about Min-ji on the street corner and the warm bundle on his knees and the forty-seven steps they had dragged Dew up.

The bandit attacked. A slanting cut from upper right, with full force.

Do-yun struck back. The first step of the Bell Strike Sword. The simplest. The slowest. A single heavy cut straight ahead, the way you swing a clapper against a bell.

The two blades met.

And Do-yun's sword rang.

It wasn't a clang. It was a tone, deep and full, like striking a bell with an open palm, and it ran through the blade into the bandit's saber and on, through the hilt, into the hand holding it. The saber trembled. It hummed. Then it shattered.

Not in two pieces. In four. The pieces flew clattering across the road, into the dust, into the stream.

The bandit stood there with an empty hilt in his hand, staring at it. Then he stared at Do-yun. Then he turned and ran, and the others ran with him, and up on the slope the archers slid away through the scree, and the one was still coughing as he ran.

The gorge went quiet. Only the stream rushed on.

Do-yun looked at his sword. It was the same bad, blunt sword as before. But it was still humming. Very faintly. He could feel it in his palm.

***

[Han Do-yun|slowly, stunned] What was that?

[Mok Yeon|from the wagon, calm] Resonance.

[Han Do-yun|shaking his head] That's the Fifth Tone. I don't even have the fourth. I can't hear lies. I can't even tell when Chil's lying, and he lies all day.

Mok Yeon climbed slowly down from the wagon. He came over, slowly, a hand on his ribs, and stopped in front of him and looked him in the face.

[Mok Yeon|earnest] The founder wrote: some climb the tones like a staircase. One after another. Others grow them like a tree. Roots first, then the trunk, and the branches whenever they like. You can't hear lies because you don't expect any. That isn't a flaw. But your body can carry what others can't. It can hold the vibration.

[Han Do-yun|quietly] You knew that.

[Mok Yeon|after a pause] I hoped it.

Do-yun looked at him. For a long time. The skinny boy with blood on his neck, who counted bandits behind rocks before anyone could see them and called out saber cuts before they came, and then simply stopped talking so Do-yun would do it alone.

[Han Do-yun|completely calm] Who are you?

Mok Yeon held his gaze.

[Mok Yeon|after a long pause] Your junior brother.

Do-yun thought about it. He thought about it for a long time, the way he thought about everything for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and sheathed his sword, and it was still humming a little as it slid in.

[Han Do-yun|good-natured, himself again] Fine. Then eat the tofu. Min-ji made too much. She always makes too much.

***

They reached Wolgok in the late afternoon, unloaded the chests, got their twelve nyang and a jug of water, and set off back before the sun went down.

Just past the town, where the road climbed a hill, they heard the bell.

It came from a mountain temple among the pines on the slope, barely visible, just a green tiled roof and a thin thread of smoke. A single stroke for evening prayers. Deep, still, with an afterglow of sound that lasted a very long time and didn't want to stop, as if the bell were listening to its own note.

Mok Yeon stood up on the wagon seat. Just like that, while they were moving, and Do-yun had to grab him by the belt to keep him from falling off.

[Han Do-yun|startled] What is it?

[Mok Yeon|flat] That's Stillness.

Do-yun needed a moment. Then he understood. The song. Dal at the stove, in the rain. Stillness hears what no one says.

[Han Do-yun|slowly] The fourth bell. It hangs in the Wolgok temple? Since when?

[Mok Yeon|quietly] I don't know.

He said it as if it were the most astonishing thing he had ever said. As if he hadn't known a person could say such a thing.

They drove on. The temple bell's resonance followed them a long way down the road, and Mok Yeon sat on the seat and kept turning around.

Do-yun drove. He had the twelve nyang in his breast pocket and the empty blue cloth in the other, and in his right palm, where he had held the sword, something still hummed a little if he paid close attention.

He thought of Min-ji. He thought about the Mid-Autumn Festival, two months away. On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival, the young people of Hacheon went down to the river with lanterns, and anyone who wanted to ask someone something asked then.

He would ask her on Mid-Autumn night.

He told no one. But he smiled the whole way home, and Mok Yeon, sitting beside him and not using Bell Ear because he had promised the Master, heard how his heart sounded anyway.

And looked, for the first time that day, as if he were afraid.
