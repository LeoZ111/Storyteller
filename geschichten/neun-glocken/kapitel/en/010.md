# Chapter 10: The Righteous Blade

Cheon Mu-gyeong never slept more than two hours at a stretch.

He had grown used to it the way you grow used to a stone in your shoe that you can't take out. He got up in the night when the pain beneath his breastbone grew too loud, and read. Or wrote letters. Or walked through the gardens of whatever house he was a guest in and watched the fish in the ponds, which didn't sleep either.

In Hacheon he was staying at the house of the Yun family.

It was a beautiful house, two courtyards deep, with a pond full of red carp and a pavilion of dark wood from which you could see the river. You could also see the warehouse row. What was left of it. Black beams jutting from the ash like the ribs of a starved animal. The smoke had cleared, but the smell lingered whenever the wind came off the water.

That afternoon, old Master Yun had knelt before him and wept. Eight hundred sacks of rice, burned. Three years' stock. Cheon Mu-gyeong had raised him up with both hands and promised that the Cheon family would make good the loss. Every sack. Master Yun had wept harder.

Eight hundred sacks of rice. Bought in spring at a quarter of the price, when the first refugees came down from the north and had to sell their stores to keep moving. Held back when the second wave came in summer. Offered at four times the price when the children in the camps outside town started eating grass. Cheon Mu-gyeong had visited old Yun three times. Three times he had asked. The third time Master Yun had offered him tea and said the market was the market.

Now the Cheon family would pay for eight hundred sacks of rice, and they would not go into Master Yun's granary but into the camps outside town. And Master Yun would be grateful. And everyone in Hacheon would know who had helped them in their need, and who had not.

No one had died. He had insisted on that.

Cheon Mu-gyeong stood in the pavilion looking at the black beams, and beneath his breastbone something throbbed, slow and deep, like a fist pounding on a door.

***

[Cheon Ui-jin|from the stairs, cheerful] Father! You're still awake.

He turned. His son was coming up the steps to the pavilion in a light sleeping robe, hair loose, a lantern in his hand. He looked like his mother when he smiled. He was nearly always smiling.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|warm] And so are you. Do you have a fever, or are you in love?

Ui-jin turned red. That was the lovely thing about him: he had never learned to hide anything. Cheon Mu-gyeong had made sure of it. He had given him the best teachers, the best books, the best sword. And he had kept him away from everything that might teach him that sometimes you have to lie.

[Cheon Ui-jin|embarrassed, happy] She said yes. Yu Seol-a. From the Nine Bells Gate. She's coming to Seoan in the eighth month. She gave me her answer today. In the market, in front of everyone.

He pulled a folded letter from his sleeve as if to read it aloud, then tucked it back again, because it occurred to him that one doesn't do that.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|smiling] The swordswoman with the fourth strike.

[Cheon Ui-jin|eager] You remember! Father, you'll see her. You'll understand what I mean. She isn't like the daughters of the great families. She fights as if her life depended on it. As if she had nothing else.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|gently] Perhaps she has nothing else.

Ui-jin paused. Then he nodded slowly, grown serious, as if his father had said something very wise. He sat on the pavilion railing, the lantern beside him, and looked across at the black beams by the river.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quieter] Terrible, the fire. I feel sorry for old Yun.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] So do I.

[Cheon Ui-jin|thoughtful] Do you know what was strange? This evening, when I was coming back, two bells rang on Unmu. Seol-a said they'd gotten a bell back from the pawnshop. A junior disciple somehow made it possible. She didn't say how. She talked about him very strangely altogether.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|casually] Strangely?

[Cheon Ui-jin|laughing] As if until yesterday she thought he was a fool and now doesn't know what to think of him. His name is Mok Yeon. Apparently he ran away from a dog in the spring.

Cheon Mu-gyeong said nothing.

He thought of the sound on the road. Two bells, one deep and one bright, and between them that hum. He thought of how quiet it had gone beneath his breastbone. Just for a moment. For the first time in months.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|after a while, fatherly] Go to bed, Ui-jin. In three days you have to stand beside me and look as if you've slept.

Ui-jin laughed, jumped off the railing, and ran down the stairs, swinging the lantern. Halfway down he turned once more.

[Cheon Ui-jin|warmly] Thank you, Father. For Seol-a. For saying yes.

Cheon Mu-gyeong raised a hand. He watched the light until it disappeared into the second courtyard.

***

The man in grey was waiting by the carp.

He stood at the edge of the pond, hands in his sleeves, looking into the dark water, and if Cheon Mu-gyeong hadn't known he was there, he would have taken him for a statue. A man of about forty-five, slender, with a clean-shaven, friendly face that recalled a village schoolteacher. His robe was a grey that looked like nothing by day and like less than nothing by night.

Hwang Ryeom. Head of the Black Lanterns.

He bowed as Cheon Mu-gyeong came down the stairs. Neither deep nor shallow. Exactly the way a craftsman bows to a client he values.

[Hwang Ryeom|quiet, polite] Patriarch.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] No one died?

[Hwang Ryeom|nodding] No one. Two watchmen with burned hands. A dog. I'm sorry about the dog.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|curt] Good.

He stepped up beside him at the pond. For a while they both looked into the water, where a red carp slid slowly out from under a lily pad and disappeared beneath it again.

[Hwang Ryeom|matter-of-fact, like a report] Two things didn't go as they should have. The first: the pawnbroker Ok's warehouse was empty. Completely. She had it cleared the day before, very quietly, with twenty porters, at night. Someone warned her.

Cheon Mu-gyeong slowly turned his head.

[Hwang Ryeom|calmly continuing] The second: my third man was followed. By a boy. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. The boy cornered him in an alley, unarmed, and disarmed him.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|incredulous] A boy disarmed one of your men.

[Hwang Ryeom|without embarrassment] With tin. He struck a row of tin sheets at the tinsmith's, in a narrow passage. My man says the sound was so loud he didn't know which way was up. He says for one breath his qi stumbled. He says he has never experienced anything like it. And he has experienced a great deal.

The pain beneath Cheon Mu-gyeong's breastbone struck the door once, hard.

[Hwang Ryeom|quieter] Then the boy hit him exactly on the tendon of the wrist. Not hard. But exactly. My man lost his token. And his pouch.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|very calm] What was in the pouch?

[Hwang Ryeom|equally calm] The map of Unmu.

It was quiet for a long time. A frog jumped into the water. The rings spread across the pond to the edge and vanished.

[Hwang Ryeom|after a while] I saw the boy. Later, from the boat. The ox from Unmu carried him up the mountain. He was bleeding from both ears. I greeted him. He looked at me as if he knew me.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|softly] Mok Yeon.

Hwang Ryeom raised an eyebrow, very slightly. That was all the surprise he ever allowed himself.

***

They went together into the rear courtyard.

There, in the last building behind the stable, was a room the Yun family used to store harness. Cheon Mu-gyeong had asked for it for the duration of his stay. For his luggage, he had said. For things he preferred to keep an eye on himself. Master Yun had hurried to give him the key.

Hwang Ryeom stopped in front of the door. He never went inside. That was part of his code, just as he never killed children and never set fires at temples.

Cheon Mu-gyeong unlocked it.

A single oil lamp burned in the room. Against the wall, on a straw pallet, sat a man. His hands were bound with soft leather straps to a ring in the wall, long enough that he could lie down and eat and drink. Beside him stood a bowl of rice and meat, half eaten, and a jug of water. He was a big man with a scar over one eye, and he had once led a gang that robbed merchants at Black Pine Pass and killed them when they resisted. He didn't look like that anymore. He looked like someone who hadn't slept in weeks, and his eyes were very bright and very empty.

He raised his head when Cheon Mu-gyeong came in. He said nothing. He had stopped saying anything a long time ago.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|friendly, almost gentle] Have you eaten? Good. Finish it all. Tomorrow there's chicken.

He knelt in front of him. He laid his right hand on the man's chest, flat, just beneath the breastbone, the same spot where he always held it on himself.

He closed his eyes and breathed in.

It wasn't a sound you could hear. It was a pull, slow and steady, like drawing water from a well. The man on the pallet stiffened, his fingers clawing into the straw, but he didn't scream. A thin, reddish haze rose from his skin, barely visible in the oil lamp's light, like breath on a cold morning, and drew itself into Cheon Mu-gyeong's palm.

Beneath Cheon Mu-gyeong's breastbone the pain grew quieter. Not silent. Never silent. Only quieter. Like a fist growing tired.

He took his hand away. The man slumped against the wall, breathing heavily.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, rising] Thank you.

He meant it. That was the worst part, and he knew it, and twelve years ago he had stopped thinking about it, because thinking changed nothing. At twenty-five he had stood on a pass and held off thirty bandits so that two hundred people could get over the mountain, and his meridians had torn like old cloth. The physicians told him he would never lift a sword again. That he would die at forty of internal bleeding. That nothing could be done.

A man had brought him a book. A man whose name he never spoke, not even in his thoughts. And in that book it said that something could be done after all.

Thirteen years had passed since then. In those thirteen years he had broken six bandit gangs, contained three plagues, absorbed two waves of refugees, and held together a province that would have fallen apart long ago without him. Thousands were alive because he was alive. And against the walls of cellars and storerooms and stables sat men like this one, all of them murderers, giving him what they had taken from others.

He believed in it. Most of the time.

He locked the door behind him. Hwang Ryeom stood exactly as before, hands in his sleeves, gaze on the dark stable.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|after a while, quietly] Ten years ago I was on Unmu. Ryu Gang was still alive. He was sick, coughing blood, but he had the Fourth Tone. He looked at me when I gave him the silver for the bell. For a long time. Back then I thought he heard something. Then he died, and I stopped thinking it.

[Hwang Ryeom|calm] His widow has the Fourth Tone too.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|nodding] I know. That's why you watch her. That's why you watch everyone who can do such things before I speak anywhere in public. And in three days I speak in public. And she'll be there.

[Hwang Ryeom|matter-of-fact] If she hears something?

Cheon Mu-gyeong looked up at the sky. The moon stood nearly full above the courtyard, and from somewhere very far away came the faint hum of a single bell that someone had brushed in the night, perhaps the wind.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm] Then you stop waiting.

Hwang Ryeom bowed.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|after a moment] And the boy. Mok Yeon. I want to know everything about him. Where he comes from. What he eats. Who he talks to. Why a boy who ran from a dog in spring disarms one of your men with tin in summer.

[Hwang Ryeom|quietly] And if he hears something?

Cheon Mu-gyeong didn't answer at once. He thought of the hum on the road. Of the quiet beneath his breastbone. Of his son on the stairs, saying thank you.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|very quietly] He's sixteen.

It wasn't an answer. Hwang Ryeom understood it anyway. He bowed a second time and left, and his grey robe vanished into the dark of the garden like smoke into the night.

Cheon Mu-gyeong stayed alone in the courtyard. His right hand lay on his chest. He stood there until the moon sank behind the stable roof, listening to the south.

But the bells on Unmu were silent.
