# Chapter 9: Two Bells

Seo Hwa-ryeong was fifty-eight years old. In her life she had sold four bells, buried a husband, and watched eleven disciples leave. She had thought there wasn't much left that could frighten her.

Then she had woken in the night because the sky over Hacheon was red, and she had listened across the mountain the way she did every night before falling back asleep. One heart after another. Dal, Chil, Seol-a. And two were missing.

She had stood at the gate barefoot, pipe clenched in her fist, and waited. She hadn't smoked. She had only waited, until at last, far down the slope on the main path, she heard the First Disciple's heavy, slow heart, and on top of it, close beside it, a second one, thin and fluttering. Like a bird that had flown into a window.

Now she stood in the doorway of the sleeping room and looked at the boy.

He sat on his straw mat, his torso bandaged, and on his neck were two dark, dried lines, one left, one right, from ear to collarbone. He looked like something a child had cut out of paper and held too close to the fire. He was holding something in his hands.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|very quietly] You promised me.

[Mok Yeon|without looking up] Yes.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|even quieter] From both ears.

[Mok Yeon|nodding] Yes.

She could have shouted at him. She wanted to. She already had the words in her mouth, the sharp ones she usually saved for merchants trying to sell her bad timber. But then he raised his head and she saw his face, and it wasn't the face of a boy who'd done something wrong and was expecting trouble.

It was the face of a man asking for something.

He held out his hands. In them lay a small black charm and a folded piece of paper.

***

She read the map three times.

She sat on the floor of the sleeping room beside him, because her knees suddenly didn't want to hold her, and held the thin paper up to the light from the window. The mountain. The courtyard. The west wall. The side gate. Bolted inside. Bolt weak. Wait.

She knew the charm. Not this one, but its kind. She had seen one thirty years ago, in the hand of a dead man pulled out of the Cheongnyu. A merchant from Mujang who had owed too many people money. Ryu Gang had explained the sign to her then, in a low voice, as if it could hear him.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|flat] The Black Lanterns.

[Mok Yeon|calm] Yes.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|shaking her head] It makes no sense. We have nothing. We have one bell and a leaking roof. Who pays assassins to watch a sect that has nothing?

He didn't answer.

She looked at him sideways. She heard his heart. It was beating calmly. Too calmly for a sixteen-year-old who had nearly been stabbed in the night. It beat like the heart of someone who had an answer to her question and didn't want to say it.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quietly] You know.

[Mok Yeon|after a long pause] Please don't tell anyone. Not at the gathering. No master, no family. No one.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|sharp] Why not? If someone sends assassins onto my mountain, I'll tell everyone who can carry a blade. That's what the gathering is for. The Cheon family has guards, the Cheon family has influence…

[Mok Yeon|calm, very clear] Because they want to know who we tell.

She stopped.

She listened. She didn't do it on purpose; it happened by itself, the way you blink when something flies at your eye. The Fourth Tone, Undertone, the false note beneath the true one. In the past twenty days she had heard it from this boy five times. When he said he had dreamed Bell Breathing. When he said he'd hurt himself on the ladder. When he said he liked to exaggerate.

Now she heard nothing. No false note. Only a sentence so true it almost hurt.

She folded the map and gave it back to him.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|after a while, rough] Fine. For now. But you tell me everything. Soon.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Soon.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|standing, the Master again] And the promise from the other day counts double. If you go down the mountain alone at night once more, I'll tie you to the bell. Upside down.

She went to the door. There she stopped.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|without turning] Do-yun says he had to carry you.

[Mok Yeon|dry] He likes doing that.

She made a sound that might have been a laugh, and went out.

***

Late in the morning a boy came up the mountain.

He was perhaps twelve, barefoot, with sooty hands, and he carried a scrap of paper that he handed to the Master with a deep bow. He said Madam Ok had sent him. He said he was to wait for an answer. Then he sat down on the bottom step and began picking his nose with great devotion.

Seo Hwa-ryeong read the message. It was only a few characters, in a quick, impatient hand.

The fortune-teller won. Come get your bell. Dew is yours. Hearth stays until year's end. I hate losing bets, but I hate losing a warehouse more. Ok.

She read it twice. Then she turned around slowly. Mok Yeon stood behind her in the courtyard, bandaged and pale, looking with great interest at a dandelion between the flagstones.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dangerously calm] What bet?

***

They borrowed the tofu seller's ox cart.

Han Do-yun went down to ask and came back with the cart, the ox, and the tofu seller's daughter, who declared that her father let no one alone with his ox, least of all a martial artist who couldn't drive his own wagon without squeaking. Do-yun was very red the whole time. Min-ji pretended not to notice.

They all went. The Master in front. Do-yun at the ox. Chil, who forgot the Sparrow Step because he had to run to keep up with the ox. Dal, who refused to ride in the cart and limped alongside the wheel, his scarred hands tucked into his sleeves. Mok Yeon, slowly, one hand on his bandaged ribs.

And Seol-a. She hadn't said anything. She had simply come along, a few steps behind the others, back straight, face unreadable.

In Madam Ok's back courtyard, Dew was already waiting. Someone had freed the bell from the tarp and washed it with water, and in the sunlight the sickly green didn't look quite so sick. Madam Ok sat on a chair under the pomegranate tree cracking seeds.

[Madam Ok|drawling, as the Master came in] Hwa-ryeong. Your boy robbed me.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|dry] I hear you played along.

[Madam Ok|shrugging] I always play along. Usually I win.

They looked at each other, two women who had known each other for thirty years and would never have called themselves friends. Then Madam Ok cracked a seed, and the shell flew in a high arc across the courtyard and landed with a bright ping right on Hearth's shoulder.

[Madam Ok|quieter] I've looked at them for five years. Every morning. Take the one before I change my mind.

***

Pulling the cart down the main street was easy.

Up the mountain, it wasn't.

The road to Unmu was wide enough for an ox cart until the last bend. There the forty-seven steps began, and there the ox stopped, looked at the steps, looked at the people, and lay down. Min-ji coaxed it. It closed its eyes.

So they built a sledge. Do-yun felled two young pines by the roadside, Dal lashed them into a runner with rope, and they lifted the bell off the cart onto the runner, all together, with a groan you could hear down in the valley. Dew was the smallest of the nine bells. It still weighed as much as three men.

Do-yun took the tow rope over his shoulder. He braced his feet on the first step and pulled.

The runner moved a hand's width.

He pulled again. The veins stood out on his neck. A hand's width.

The Master got behind the bell and pushed. So did Chil, with both hands and a red face. Dal jammed a wooden wedge under the runner to keep it from sliding back. Mok Yeon braced his shoulder against the bronze, on the side without the bandage, and felt his ribs protest.

Third step. Fourth.

Seol-a stood at the side and watched.

At the seventh step the wedge slipped. The runner jerked backward, Chil screamed, Do-yun threw himself against it with a roar that sounded like an ox's roar, deep and long. Mok Yeon heard it and froze. For an instant he was back inside the bell, and outside the lanterns were burning.

Then a hand reached for the bronze beside his.

A slender, pale hand with calluses on the fingertips, from eleven years of the sword.

Seol-a said nothing. She didn't look at him. She braced herself against the bell beside him, and her qi ran clean and strong through her arms, and the runner stopped sliding.

Eighth step. Ninth.

Down on the street, people had stopped to watch. At first they laughed. A beggar sect dragging a bell up a mountain as if it were treasure. Then they stopped laughing. The old woman with the radish cart came up the steps and put her bony hands on the runner and pushed, and it did no good at all, and she pushed anyway. A porter from the harbor followed her. Then two fisher boys. Then the farmhand from Pyo's agency, who recognized Mok Yeon, went red, and pitched in anyway.

Twenty-first step. Thirty-second.

At the forty-seventh they all lay in the courtyard among the moss and dandelions, gasping, and Dew stood in the middle of them on her runner of young pines, green and blotchy and home.

Chil laughed first. Then Do-yun, his tumbling-staircase laugh. Then Min-ji, who didn't want to. Then, very softly, so softly that only Mok Yeon heard it because he was lying beside her, Seol-a.

***

Dal hung it himself.

He climbed into the tower on his bad knees and let no one help him. He had spent the whole afternoon rubbing the bronze with vinegar and wood ash until the white crusts were gone and the deep old green showed through again. He had checked every link of the chain. With a pulley Do-yun had rigged from rope and a wagon wheel, they hauled the bell up piece by piece, and Dal stood up in the beams and guided it with his scarred hands onto the second hook.

The hook beside Dawn.

When it was hanging, he stood there a long time, one hand on the bell's shoulder. Nobody called him down.

The sun set. The mist rose out of the valley, slowly, as it did every evening, filling the hollows with white. The Master stood in the courtyard, pipe in hand, unlit.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, calling up] Dal. Ring Dew. Mok Yeon, ring Dawn. Together.

Mok Yeon climbed the tower.

He stood beneath Dawn, and Dal stood beneath Dew, and they looked at each other, the old man with the burned hands and the boy with blood on his neck. Then they reached for the ropes.

Mok Yeon breathed. Nine times. He felt the stream in his belly, he felt the whine in his ears, he felt his ribs. He held the tenth breath.

They pulled.

Dawn spoke first, deep and full, the note he had been striking every morning for twenty days. And then, half a breath later, Dew answered.

Dew was higher. Brighter. A note like water falling from a leaf. And when it settled over Dawn's deep tone, something happened that Mok Yeon had never heard in forty-one years, because in forty-one years he had never had two bells. The two notes didn't fight. They sought each other out. They swung around each other like two birds circling in flight, and between them arose a third tone that neither bell could make alone, a hum deeper than Dawn and brighter than Dew that didn't come from the bronze but from the air in between.

One bell is a note. Two bells are…

He didn't know what two bells were. He had never known. He stood beneath Dawn, rope in hand, and the sound passed through him, through his ribs, through his ears, and for a moment the whine in his left ear went completely silent.

The sound rolled down the mountain, over the forty-seven steps, over the mist, over the roofs of Hacheon, over the river where smoke still drifted from the burned warehouses. On the main street, people stopped and looked up.

Down in the courtyard, Seo Hwa-ryeong had turned around. She stood with her back to the tower, looking into the mist, the way she had once looked into a pomegranate tree five years ago.

No one went to her. Not Mok Yeon either. He heard what she was doing, and he let her.

***

At that same hour, on the road from Seoan, an hour north of Hacheon, a carriage came to a stop.

It was a plain carriage, lacquered black, without a crest. Four riders accompanied it. The coachman hadn't stopped the horses on his own. A voice from inside had ordered it, quietly, and he had obeyed without asking, because one obeyed that voice.

The man in the carriage had pushed the curtain aside.

He was thirty-eight, with a calm, kind face and grey streaks at the temples that made him look older than he was. He sat very straight. His right hand lay on his chest, flat, just beneath the breastbone, as if pressing back something that wanted out.

He was listening.

From the south, over the hills, through the evening mist, came a sound. Two bells. One deep and one bright, and between them a hum that hung in the air like warmth above a fire.

Cheon Mu-gyeong closed his eyes.

Beneath his hand, beneath the breastbone, where for thirteen years a pain had lived that kept him awake at night, there was silence for a moment.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|softly, almost tender] How beautiful.

He stayed sitting until the sound had faded. Then he let the curtain fall.

[Cheon Mu-gyeong|calm, to those outside] Drive on.

The carriage moved off. And in the shadow by the roadside, where a man in grey had been waiting all along with his hands in his sleeves, a fifth figure set out and followed it.
