# Chapter 28: Wedding

Han Do-yun had driven off three bandits at Black Pine Pass, held a gate against fourteen murderers, and pulled a bell out of a river. On the morning of his wedding he couldn't tie his own belt.

[Han Do-yun|desperate, ties in his hands] It won't close. It closed yesterday. Why won't it close today?

[Mok Yeon|calm] Your hands are shaking.

[Han Do-yun|indignant] My hands never shake.

Mok Yeon took the ties from him and fastened the belt. It was a red silk sash from Madam Ok, and Do-yun wore a robe of the same color that the tailor in Hacheon had sewn in three days and that pulled across the shoulders. His hair was short as always, but Dal had rubbed something into it that smelled of sesame, and it shone.

Do-yun looked down at himself.

[Han Do-yun|quietly] I look like a red lantern.

[Mok Yeon|earnest] Like a very large, very happy red lantern.

Do-yun looked at him. Then he laughed his tumbling-staircase laugh, and his hands stopped shaking.

***

The courtyard was full of people.

More than at the river bell. This time they'd been invited. Min-ji's family, which seemed to consist entirely of tofu sellers, uncles and aunts and cousins who all had the same snub nose. Madam Ok in green, with a new ring on her thumb she showed to everyone. The old woman with the radish cart, who had brought a basket of radishes as a gift and insisted the ox get some too. The farmhand from Pyo's agency. Two fisher boys from Bell Bend. Old Gong, whom Chil had led up that morning and who now sat on a stool beneath the tower, smiling.

And at the edge, by the wall, in black, with a small package under her arm: Baek Ha-seon.

She hadn't been invited. She had come anyway. She handed Do-yun the package with a bow exactly as deep as protocol required and said: A gift. For the house. Do-yun opened it. Inside lay a brush. A good one, weasel hair, in a bamboo case.

[Baek Ha-seon|matter-of-fact] So you can write your own letters.

Do-yun stared at her. Then he turned as red as his robe.

[Han Do-yun|stammering] I can't write very well.

[Baek Ha-seon|calm] I know. I read your statement. You signed with three characters and got one of them wrong. Practice.

She went back to the wall. Her clerk, standing behind her, looked as if he might weep with emotion.

***

Min-ji came up the forty-seven steps when the sun stood above the mist.

She wore red. A robe with wide sleeves and a collar of gold thread that had belonged to her mother and her mother's mother before that. Her hair was pinned up with jade pins Madam Ok had lent her and which she had to give back, Madam Ok had insisted, with a look that left no doubt. She wore no veil. She had said she wanted to see where she was going.

She came through the gate. She saw Do-yun. She stopped.

Do-yun stood in the middle of the courtyard among the moss and dandelions, in his too-tight red robe, staring at her as if someone had hit him with a bell clapper.

[Bang Chil|whispering, far too loudly, to Mok Yeon] He's not breathing. Mok Yeon. Do-yun isn't breathing.

Mok Yeon listened. It was true. Do-yun was holding his breath. The tenth. The pause in which nothing happens.

He seemed to have found it for the first time without a hand.

***

The Master married them.

There was no priest on Unmu and no temple, and Min-ji's family didn't care so long as someone spoke the words and someone else wrote them down. The Master stood before the altar in the main hall, beneath the two missing tiles, through which the sun fell today instead of rain, and Do-yun and Min-ji stood before her, and the Master turned her head so she could hear them both with her left ear.

She didn't speak long. She spoke about bells.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|calm, too loud] The founder said one bell is a note. And two bells are two notes and a third that comes out of the air between them. That neither can make alone.

She looked at the two of them.

[Seo Hwa-ryeong|quieter] That's all I know about marriage. My husband and I needed twenty years to understand it. You're cleverer than we were. I hope you'll only need ten.

Then she asked Do-yun whether he would take Min-ji, and Do-yun said Yes so loudly the bells in the tower hummed. And then she asked Min-ji, and Min-ji looked at Do-yun for a long time with that determined round face and the snub nose, and then she said:

[Min-ji|quietly, with a smile she couldn't hold back] Idiot.

It was a yes. Everyone knew it. The inspector's clerk wrote it down. With exactly that word.

***

Then they rang the bells.

Mok Yeon, Seol-a, and Chil, each beneath a bell, and the Master below in the courtyard keeping time with her pipe stem. Dawn, Dew, Current. Not the chord. Something else. A peal Dal had taught them the evening before, from memory, the way he had built Current's mold from memory. The wedding peal of the Nine Bells Gate. It hadn't been rung in twenty years.

It went like this: Dawn, twice. Then Dew, once. Then Current, long. Then all three together. And then from the beginning again, each time a little faster, like a heart beating faster when it sees someone.

Down in the courtyard the tofu sellers danced. Madam Ok danced with the farmhand from Pyo's agency, who looked as if he didn't know what was happening to him. The old woman with the radish cart danced alone, arms raised. Old Gong sat beneath the tower, hands on his knees, swaying in time.

And Do-yun and Min-ji stood in the middle of the courtyard and didn't dance. They simply stood there, forehead to forehead, the way disciples rest their foreheads against the bell to feel it hum.

***

Cheon Ui-jin came in the afternoon.

He came alone, on foot, up the forty-seven steps, with a bundle on his back. He had no horse. He looked tired. More tired than in the ninth month, when he rode off on the lame horse. There were shadows under his eyes.

But he smiled as he came through the gate. He bowed to the bride and groom. He gave Min-ji a bolt of silk, pale green, like water under ice, and Do-yun a bottle of rice wine from the Cheon family cellar, twenty years old. Do-yun took it as if it would break if he looked at it.

Then Ui-jin looked around. From the tower, Mok Yeon saw his gaze travel across the courtyard. Over the dancers. Over the inspector by the wall. Until he found Seol-a.

She was standing at the stable door. She had heard him coming. Of course she had.

He went to her. Slowly. He took the bundle off his back. He opened it. He took something out.

Mok Yeon saw it from the tower. Something red. Small. A tassel. Red as dried blood.

[Cheon Ui-jin|quietly] The pawnbroker at the north market showed it to me. He said a young woman with a sword pawned it in the eighth month for two coppers. He didn't know why. He thought she was mad.

He held it out to her.

[Cheon Ui-jin|very quietly] I redeemed it. For two coppers. And then I wondered why someone would pawn the tassel off her sword. On a day when a man in grey was sitting in the pawnshop drinking tea. The pawnbroker mentioned the man in grey too. He was there often, he said. He always asked about letters.

Seol-a didn't take the tassel. She looked at him. Mok Yeon heard her heart all the way up in the tower.

[Cheon Ui-jin|barely audible] Seol-a. I've seen a man in grey in Seoan. He's a steward with us. For ten years. He was with me on Unmu when we brought you the beam.

He swallowed.

[Cheon Ui-jin|whispering] Explain it to me. Please. I didn't collect Chestnut, I waited, I lied to my father to be here, for the first time in my life I lied to him. Explain it to me.

***

They went to the training ground, under the old pine, as the sun went down.

Mok Yeon listened. He shouldn't have. He did anyway. He stood in the tower beneath Dawn, his hand on the bronze, and listened through the bronze, which carries everything said near it.

Seol-a told him.

She didn't tell it well. She faltered. She started over twice. She told him about the family dinner and the laugh and the screaming beneath it. She told him about the cellar behind the stable, the iron door, the four heartbeats, the red haze above the threshold. The thank you. The night in the ancestral hall, Dusk on its wooden trestle, the man in grey and the Patriarch, and a date. The fifteenth of the ninth month.

Ui-jin didn't interrupt her.

When she finished, it was quiet for a long time. Down in the courtyard the wedding guests were laughing. Someone was playing a flute, badly. Do-yun was shouting something, happily.

[Cheon Ui-jin|finally, very quietly] You heard something.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Yes.

[Cheon Ui-jin|flat] Beneath a laugh. Something no one else hears. With an art you've had for three months.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible] Yes.

[Cheon Ui-jin|louder, and his voice broke] And for that my father is supposed to be a monster? The man who stood on a pass at twenty-five? Who lies awake every night because he's in pain? Who taught me to ride and to write and to pick up an old woman's radishes? Because of something you claim to have heard?

Seol-a said nothing.

[Cheon Ui-jin|desperate] The cellar behind the stable is where we keep the harness! I was down there a thousand times as a child!

[Yu Seol-a|very quietly] When were you last down there, Ui-jin?

Silence.

Mok Yeon heard Ui-jin's heart. It was beating fast and loud, and beneath it there was nothing. No undertone. Only pain, as open as everything about him. And then, very slowly, something else. Something that didn't lie beneath but pushed its way in, like water into a crack. A hesitation.

[Cheon Ui-jin|barely audible] Since I was twelve. My father said the door stuck. He put in a new lock.

***

He turned and walked away.

Not toward the gate. Toward the tower. Mok Yeon heard him come up the ladder, fast, unsteady, and then he stood at the top between the three bells with a face Mok Yeon had never seen on him. Not open. Not friendly. Torn.

[Cheon Ui-jin|hoarse] You. You knew too. You said it the way she says it. I know. Two months ago at the gate. You said: I know. When I said my father always helps.

Mok Yeon looked at him. He couldn't lie to him. He had never been able to lie to anyone who looked at him like that.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] Yes.

[Cheon Ui-jin|whispering] How?

Mok Yeon said nothing. What could he say? I met him once at seventy-nine on the steps of the Alliance Hall in Mujang, and he snapped my meridians like old strings, and it took me forty years to understand who he is. And you fell from your horse at twenty-seven, on a clear day, on a mountain road.

[Cheon Ui-jin|bitter, after a long pause] You're all mad. You hear things beneath laughs and beneath heartbeats and beneath bells, and you believe what you hear is truer than what someone does. My father gave rice to three thousand people in Hacheon. I saw that. With my eyes. Not beneath anything.

He turned. He climbed down the ladder. Down in the courtyard he walked past the dancers, past Do-yun, who called something to him, past Min-ji, past the Master, who turned her head to hear him with her left ear and heard nothing, because he said nothing.

At the gate stood Seol-a. She was holding Chestnut's reins.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Take him. He's your horse. It's a long way.

Ui-jin looked at the horse. Chestnut snorted and nudged his shoulder with his nose, the way he nudged Seol-a's shoulder in the stable every day.

[Cheon Ui-jin|flat] Keep him. I'll come for him when you explain to me how you can be so wrong.

He went down the steps. On foot. He didn't turn around.

Seol-a stood at the gate, reins in hand. Then she called after him. Not loudly. But the bronze in the tower carried it, and Mok Yeon heard every word.

[Yu Seol-a|quiet, clear] Go into the cellar, Ui-jin. Behind the stable. You don't need the Fourth Tone for that. You only need a door.

He didn't stop. But on the twenty-third step, Mok Yeon heard, his heart skipped a beat.

***

The wedding guests celebrated into the night. Dal had cooked enough for two hundred, and it barely stretched to sixty. Madam Ok lost a bet to Old Gong; neither of them would say what it was about. Chil fell asleep under the table for twenty at some point, with an owl feather in his hair.

At midnight Do-yun and Min-ji stood in the tower, just the two of them, and rang Dawn once, very softly, together, four hands on one rope.

Mok Yeon sat below on the wall and listened. Beside him sat Seol-a with the red tassel in her hand, which Ui-jin had laid on the step by the gate after all before he left. She hadn't tied it to her sword. She just held it.

[Yu Seol-a|quietly] Will he go?

Mok Yeon thought of a young man standing in a familiar room who notices that a piece of furniture has been moved. Who hadn't gone into a cellar in ten years because his father said the door stuck. Who had lied for the first time in his life to be here.

[Mok Yeon|honest] I don't know. I don't know the future anymore.

[Yu Seol-a|barely audible] What do you hear?

He listened north. Over the hills, the fields, the dark road. He heard nothing. Seoan was too far. No Bell Ear in the world reached that far.

But at Seol-a's throat, on the red cord, the little bell from Storm was humming. Very softly. Without anyone touching it.

[Mok Yeon|quietly] I hear that you're hoping.
